The Heliotrope

A walnut viewer for 7 × 3½ in stereographs. A gilt Sun covers a ground-glass skylight; lift it away and daylight falls on the card. The viewer twists onto a gilt pedestal whose drawer holds the collection.

Design notes

Using it
  • Choose. Pull the drawer. Cards stand on their long edge facing you, like a card catalogue, held upright by a sliding follower; dividers take handwritten labels.
  • Raise the Sun. Lift the cover off with both hands, tip it toward you until it stands, and drop its lower edge into the gilt crest at the back. The Sun now faces you, upright, and the skylight is open.
  • Set the card. Drop it picture-first into the slot just in front of the crest. It falls onto a silk ribbon and stops on the gate sill, exactly on the optical axis.
  • Focus. Turn each eyecup's knurled ring. Three turns cover the whole range; each eye focuses separately.
  • Put away. Pull a ribbon loop and the card rises out of the slot. Cover goes back on; four magnets seat it.
  • Moving it. Hold the body under the cornice, turn a twelfth anticlockwise and lift. It stands flat on any table.
Optics

Your 38 mm, f 150 mm biconvex lenses work as simple magnifiers. With the card exactly one focal length away, each picture appears at infinity, so the eyes relax as if looking at a distant view.

The two pictures on a standard card sit about 76 mm apart centre to centre, while eyes are usually 58–70 mm apart. Brewster cut his lenses in half to add prism. Here the whole lenses sit on 76 mm centres instead. Light from a point at the focal plane leaves the lens parallel to the line through the lens centre, so direction depends only on where the lens is, not where the eye is. With lens centres over picture centres, both eyes look straight ahead. Each eye looks through the inner part of its lens; at the ~50 mm eye relief the field still covers the full ±14° picture.

The helicoid moves the lens from 158 to 135 mm from the card: about +0.3 D to −0.7 D, plus whatever the eye accommodates. A septum stops each eye seeing the other picture.

Light

The skylight is 105 mm long and runs from 27 mm behind the lens board to 10 mm in front of the card. Light from its front end reaches the card only ~25° off normal, so an overhead lamp or window lights the picture evenly without glare off the card's gloss.

Paint the card chamber matte white and the front half matte black. The glare baffle under the roof keeps the bright glass out of the lenses' view without clipping the picture.

The end ground glass sits 4 mm behind the card, one focal length from the lenses. With the slot empty, the lenses act as a camera obscura and throw two small upside-down images of the room onto it.

The bayonet mount

A brass boss on the pedestal carries three lugs. The socket under the viewer has three matching notches, a gallery the lugs turn into, and a solid stop at the end of the 30° turn. Set the viewer down turned 30° anticlockwise, let it drop the 9.5 mm onto the boss, and turn it clockwise until it stops square.

In the locked position the lugs sit under a 5 mm lip, so the viewer can't lift, slide or tip off. Three pairs of 6 × 3 mm magnets hold it at the square position so it won't wander. Nothing needs adjusting and nothing can wear loose.

Making it

Wood. The body is ½″ (12.7 mm) walnut. The walls taper 10.1° in plan, so the lens board and end wall have matching bevelled ends. The roof is two laminations, the lower one stepped back 4 mm to make the glass ledge. STLs for the wood parts are full-size templates.

Printed and gilt. Print the Sun, medallions, cornice, frame, crest, column, consoles and knob in resin, or in PLA at 0.1 mm. Then gesso, bole and water-gild, or use oil size and leaf. Print the eyepieces in PLA or PETG, upright. Their 3-start threads have 0.4 mm radial clearance and need no supports.

Buy. Two panes of 2–2.5 mm ground glass (templates included), 10 mm silk ribbon and felt for the drawer runners. The Prototype kit view lists the screws, heat-set inserts, magnets and M8 rod that hold everything together.
