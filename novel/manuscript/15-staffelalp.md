# 15. Staffelalp

He was at the station before me, of course. I came up the last of the west path in the ash-light with my heart going harder than the grade asked, and where the moraine crest leveled there was a smell of pipe smoke on the air, and a shape sitting on the center stone of his own masonry with its hands on its knees, watching the ice, and without turning it said, in a voice I had been hearing in a red-lit cellar for six weeks:

"Mind the datum, Mr. Joiner. The lady is particular."

Ambrose Fell at fifty-seven was a long spare man gone to whipcord and weather, in a suit of gray Norfolk cloth worn to the color of the moraine, so that sitting still he was very nearly a feature of it. What the portrait in my head had not supplied was the stillness. Most men, waiting, spend the waiting; they shift, they mend the fire, they inventory their pockets. Fell sat the way the mountain's own furniture sat. When he rose and turned at last, unfolding to a height within an inch of my own, I met a face like a wintered orchard, all the softness rendered out of it and what remained arranged in lines a man could trust, and two pale eyes that went over me as light goes over a plate: once, entire, keeping everything.

"You'll have questions," he said. "They'll keep till the hut. The light won't." He nodded at the ice, at the arch of the cave far down the tongue, filling with the last blue of the day. "You brought the viewer. Rack the pair you trust and tell me what's changed since the ninth of August of last year, and take your time, because everything I do next depends on the answer, and so does everything you do."

That is how Ambrose Fell said good evening, after thirty-one years of waiting and eleven months of baiting a road across two countries, and I want the reader to understand that I recognized it, from the inside, at once. It was not coldness. It was the grammar of a man to whom work had been for so long the one language with no lies in it that he opened every important conversation in it, as other men open in prayer or in weather. I had grown up watching another man do it with lumber. *Take the far clamp.* There are people you meet already knowing the way in.

I had carried the day's print up with me, made that morning from a plate Clemenz had exposed at the station to my order the week I was in Italy, and I racked it now against Fell's own last plate, the 9th of August, 1896: two views of one subject, three hundred and sixty days apart, and I stood on the darkening moraine with my eyes in the hood while the man who had invented the method waited with his pipe.

A year of ice stood up in the walnut dark. The whole tongue had come forward and down, the crevasse fields translated wholesale, last summer's surface folded under and gone; and the bright grain, the fleck that had ridden every fusion since July of '96, was no longer a fleck. At the wall above the cave mouth, deep in the blue, it had opened into a little constellation, five points, six, strung close on a rising line like harness fittings along a strap, and below the constellation, fainter, a seventh gleam, longer, dull, which did not read as metal, and which I did not then name aloud, and which Fell did not require me to.

"Displacement of the group against the cave lip," he said. "In feet. Estimate."

"Forty. Perhaps forty-five."

"Miss Forsyth's mid-line rate for the coming fortnight?"

"Fourteen inches a day, rising with this weather. She closes her envelopes on the twentieth, five days' grace either side."

"So," said Fell, and knocked out his pipe against his heel with the finality of a man ruling a line under a column, and the sparks went down among the stones. "Thirty-one years, and it comes to a fortnight, and the fortnight has three photographers in it, one of them a thief. Come to the hut, Mr. Joiner. The milk's yours; I've had a year of it."

* * *

The cheese hut above Staffel was stone below and larch above, one room, and he had it ordered like a ship's cabin: cot, spirit stove, plate cases racked and strapped along the windless wall, the great field camera standing under an oilcloth like a hooded bird, and on the shelf where the herdsmen keep their saints, three books and a folding frame that I knew the pattern of, shut, with its brass hook toward the wall. He fed me bread and hard cheese and the shepherd's milk, and himself mostly tobacco, and while I ate he took my report, all of it, in the order I chose, interrupting exactly twice.

The first interruption came when I reached Forsyth's conversion, her father's window, the letter gone to Edinburgh. He was still for a moment, his pipe hand resting on his knee.

"She showed you the tables herself? Voluntarily?"

"She audited your method first. Then she showed me everything she had."

"She sent me down that moraine, last July, in fifteen words," said Fell. "Every one of them deserved. I have thought since: there is the one person in the valley doing this thing for its right reason, and the reason I gave her for mine was a lens." He repacked the pipe, slower than the pipe needed. "Her father wrote one true sentence about that mountain, and they hung it round his neck and drowned him with it. *Render its account.* When you go down, tell her—" and then he stopped himself, with the first smile I had from him, a dry small thing that used few muscles and meant more for it, "—no. I'll tell her. I've rehearsed apologies to that family for a year; hers is the shortest and I'll deliver it standing."

The second interruption came at the cellar, the re-ordered prints, the Colonel's card. He heard the name Prewitt without any change I could see by lamplight, which was itself informative, most men's faces doing something with contempt, and his doing nothing at all: a scale taking a weight it has weighed before.

"I know the Colonel of old," was all he banked it with. "Memphis. Lucerne. The world. Go on."

When I had finished, the letter from my mother included, because he asked what my traffic was and I found I would no sooner lie to him than to her, he sat a while with his back against the plate cases, in the lamp's small weather, and then he paid for my report with his own, as he had paid the widow Ghiglione's feeding with portraits, exactly, and without being asked, and I will set down what he said as near his words as forty years permit, because it was the account of the account, and I was the only man who ever heard it.

"You'll have had my history from Considine, so I'll not waste the lamp on the firm. The contract was fair robbery and I signed it awake; a man who sells his eyes at thirty-eight and cries at sixty is crying at arithmetic he did himself. That's not what this is." He nodded at the wall, through it, at the ice invisible in the dark outside. "I'll give you what this is, and I'll give it to you straight, because you printed the eleven and came up the west path without the firm in your pockets, and that has earned the straight version once, after which we won't speak of it again.

"I stood on this moraine in June of 1878 with a shot list, a boy from Chamonix, and the finest light in Europe, and I made the mountain by every station your book has in it now, and one morning an old herdsman watched me work an hour and then said, in the patois, thinking I had no German: *he takes the hat and leaves the head.* I paid it no mind for years. I was busy; the sets sold; you were raised on them, you tell me, and you were, I've seen your Rigi plate, you were raised well." He said that without ceremony, laying it down like a tool passed handle first, and went on before it could be picked up. "But the sentence worked in me, as true sentences do. I have made this mountain ten thousand times, Mr. Joiner. Every card says underneath it, in type no lens finds: *taken by no one, of the outside of things.* The hat, and never once the head. And all those years, under the postcard weather, the head was here." He touched the stone floor of the hut with two fingers. "In the dark. Coming down at nine inches a day. The one appointment in this whole valley that was not scenery. Croz and Hudson and the boy Hadow were carried down by men, and lie under names. The fourth was carried down by the mountain itself, thirty-one years, the slowest cortège in the world and no one walking in it.

"Then Marianne died, and I'll not give you that, except the one piece that belongs to this story. She knew the herdsman's sentence; I'd told it her years before, in the way you hand your wife the splinter you can't reach. And the last winter, in Nice, she said to me: *you've photographed everything and witnessed nothing, and you know the difference, and there's time.*" He looked at the lamp flame with an orchard face. "There's time. Well. She was right by eleven months, on her own account. And when the firm wrote me about the great re-making, I thought: yes. I began there. I'll finish there. I'll do the list, for wages, with the hat in it, every number, and then I will stay past the season, and stand at the bottom of that ice on the morning it renders its account, and be the walker in the cortège, and make one photograph of the outside of things on the one morning the outside and the inside are the same thing. And it will be witnessed, and it will be mine." The pale eyes came up from the flame to me, level. "Not the firm's, because it will be made on my own glass, bought with my own silver, on my own time, which the contract, I have read it forty times, does not reach. Whether it is ever sold, or shown, or seen by another living soul, is a question I have not resolved and may not resolve until I am standing in front of it. But it will carry my name somewhere on it, if only scratched in the corner where the mount hides it, because a man should sign the one thing he witnessed, Mr. Joiner, even if he signs it to the dark."

The stove ticked. Outside, far off, the ice said its one dry word.

"That's the straight version," said Ambrose Fell, and stood, and took the second cup down off the shelf and filled it with the last of the shepherd's milk, and in handing it to me remarked, in the voice of a man closing a ledger and opening an almanac, "Now. You'll take the cot; I don't use it in fair weather. At four we'll walk my near line, there's a reading wants two pairs of hands. And when we're down, you'll present me to Miss Forsyth for the apology, and we three will sit down like a board of directors and decide what's to be done about the twentieth of August, the family's answer, and the Colonel of relics. He'll come at money first. He always came at money first. The trouble with the Colonel," and he turned the lamp down with two fingers, unhurried, "is that the year he finds a thing money won't buy is the year he reaches for the other shelf."

"What's on the other shelf?"

"The newspapers," said Fell, in the dark, "Goodnight, Mr. Joiner. You did well by the eleven. I could weep for the state of your wash water, but the printing was sound."

And Clemenz's message, which I delivered into that dark because I had promised, the chair, the room, the bill rendered only because firms require bills, was received in so long a silence that I had taken it at last for sleep, until there came, from the corner where the stove ticked, dry as the striking of a match:

"I know. Imboden sent my winter coat to Vernazza by the November post. Wrapped in the hotel's own paper." A pause. "Villages," said the voice, as a man says the name of a currency the banks have never listed, and the stove ticked on, and we slept, the two of us, at the head of the cortège route, with the plate cases strapped against the wall like the crew of the thing, asleep standing up.
