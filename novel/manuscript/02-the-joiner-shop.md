# 2. The Joiner Shop

The eastbound out of Chicago would not leave San Bernardino without me until Thursday, which gave me a day and two nights at home, and home was Highland, eight miles up the valley, where the orange groves run out against the first rock of the mountains and stop as sharply as if a surveyor had drawn the line between two kinds of ambition.

My father's shop stood behind the house, under a pepper tree, with JOINER lettered on the door in gold leaf he had laid himself. The sign was older than I was and it said everything the family owned worth saying. My father had been born with no surname a court would recognize, in Tennessee, in 1845, as another man's property. He had walked off that property at seventeen, been a teamster for the Union, then a soldier of the 55th Massachusetts, and after the war he had stayed in Boston and apprenticed himself to a firm of finish carpenters, and when at last a clerk in a records office asked him what name he meant to carry, he gave the man his trade. He liked to say he had considered Cabinetmaker and rejected it as putting on airs.

In Boston he had married my mother, which was legal there and illegal in most other places the two of them might have thought to live, my mother being white, a schoolteacher out of Ohio with a spine you could have squared lumber against. They came west in 1870 on the theory, my mother's, that a new country would have less time to mind other people's business. The theory proved optimistic in the usual ways. Still, the valley needed finish work more than it needed grief. The men putting up mansion after mansion down in Redlands on orange money wanted mantels and paneling and stair rails of a quality that did not care who supplied it, and my father's prices were fair and his miters were shut tight as scripture, and by the time I was old enough to sweep shavings the shop had a waiting list and the family had a piano.

I got down from the local at the Highland depot Wednesday noon with two equipment cases and a valise, and found my father in the shop gluing up a tabletop, and stood in the doorway a moment before he saw me, because a man at glue-up cannot be interrupted and both of us would have been embarrassed by how glad we were.

"Take the far clamp," he said, when he saw me. So I set my cases down and took the far clamp, and we worked the panel true together, and only when the last screw was snugged did he straighten up and look at me properly. "They feeding you, out at that hotel?"

"The waiters were. The dining room had reservations."

He grunted at the joke, which was his highest award for one, and wiped his hands. "Your mother's at the school till three. There's ham."

* * *

Over the ham I told him about Chicago, as much as SAY NOTHING allowed, which I decided meant everything, since the instruction had plainly been drafted with stockholders in mind and not fathers. I told him Fell had gone silent in Switzerland with the firm's greatest property half made, and that I was being sent east to be told why, and that I believed, from the shape of the telegram, that after Chicago there would be a steamship in it.

My father ate for a while.

"That's the man made your mountain," he said at last.

"Number 87. Yes."

"Never did put his name on it."

"The firm doesn't put names on anything. The firm is the artist."

"Mm," said my father, who had signed the underside of every drawer he had built since 1866. He got up and poured coffee for us both, and stood with his back against the sink in a manner I knew meant a speech was assembling, a speech being for him anything upward of three sentences. "When a man leaves work half done," he said, "it's the work quit him, or he quit the work, or somebody quit him of it. You'll want to know which, before you stand in his place. Men get strange about the last of a thing." He looked into his coffee. "Especially the good ones."

I have been paid for advice many times since, and given it, and I have never heard the business of that summer set out more completely than my father set it out before I had so much as bought my ticket. I did not understand this until later, which is the natural fate of advice.

* * *

My mother came home at three with chalk on her cuff and a composition book under her arm, kissed me, held me off at arm's length to conduct her inspection, and said, "You've been letting hotels feed you in the kitchen."

"Only the better hotels."

"Hollis."

"It's a long fight, Ma, and I had plates to make."

She let it go, in the sense that she folded it and put it away in the drawer where she kept things that were not finished being fought about. My mother had spent thirty years teaching the valley's children grammar, and the campaign had left her with an exactness that made her, among other things, the best caption critic I ever had. That evening I showed my parents the proofs of the Golden Shore set, passing the cards one at a time into the walnut stereoscope on the parlor table, the same instrument, its hood rebuilt by my father in '79 after the original split, through which I had first seen Venice, and the pyramids, and Number 87. My mother read each proposed caption aloud the way a judge reads an indictment.

"'The world-famed Hotel del Coronado, whose incomparable situation,'" she read, and stopped. "Whose. The situation doesn't belong to the hotel, the hotel is *in* the situation."

"The caption department is very attached to 'whose.'"

"The caption department," she said, "was raised by wolves."

My father, at the viewer, had gone slow and quiet in the manner of a man actually looking, and he stayed a long time on number nine, the hotel at seven in the morning with the light coming low across the bay. When he set it down he said, "That's good glasswork at the rail there," meaning the turned balusters of the veranda, in deep relief in the near plane, each one round as a thing you could close your hand on. Other men praised my skies. My father praised my balusters. Between the two I built my whole idea of what a view owes the person holding it.

There is one more thing from that night worth setting down. When the cards were put away my mother stayed by the lamp, and I could see her working up to a subject, and I supposed it would be the ocean crossing, or my clothes for it.

"You'll be over there among strangers a long while, by the sound of it," she said. "I'll want letters. Real ones. Not weather."

"You'll have them."

"And Hollis." She looked at me over the top of her spectacles, a gesture she performed with the authority of long service. "Over there, I'm told, they will mostly not know what to make of you. A great many of them have never seen an American of any description. You'll be a curiosity before you're anything else."

"I'm a curiosity here, Ma."

"Here," she said, "you are a Joiner," and she tapped the table once, as though entering the fact in a ledger, and took up her composition books, and that was the end of the sitting.

* * *

Thursday morning my father drove me down to the San Bernardino depot behind the bay mare, through the groves, with the mountains standing up hard on our left in their spring snow. I had grown up under those mountains and had photographed them perhaps two hundred times, and I want to record honestly what I felt looking at them that morning, with a ticket to Chicago in my breast pocket and beyond it, unpurchased and shining, a ticket to the Alps. I felt disloyal. It is a small, absurd feeling and I have since learned that every traveler owns a version of it, but there it was, riding along between us in the buggy. San Gorgonio hung white over the valley like a debt.

At the depot my father carried the heavier case to the platform himself, which the porter permitted with professional sympathy, one tradesman to another. The California Limited came down out of the pass on time, brakes singing, all varnish and importance.

"Mind the good ones," my father said. It had cost him the whole drive to compress the speech to that.

"I will."

He shook my hand as if we were concluding a sale, and then, with the handshake still going, put his left hand on my shoulder, which in the full vocabulary of Ezra Joiner was an embrace with weeping. I boarded, found my seat among some cattlemen and a honeymoon couple, and put my face to the glass. He was already walking back along the platform, not turning, one hand up in the air behind him in farewell, a man with glue drying at home.

The train ran east through the groves, and the whole car filled with the smell of orange blossom, which is a smell I have met since on three continents, in soaps and tonics and once in a cathedral, and which has never once meant anything but this: the last two miles of the San Bernardino Valley, going away.

Then Cajon Pass took us, and the desert had the train the rest of the day.
