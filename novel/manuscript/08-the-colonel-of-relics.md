# 8. The Colonel of Relics

July opened the season the way a curtain opens a play. Inside of ten days the empty street became a promenade, the hotels put on their linen and their orchestras, sixty mules a day came up from the railhead under trunks with steamship labels, and the guides stood outside the Monte Rosa each morning in their black coats, being engaged, like cabs with summits. English came up in flocks, as foretold. So did Americans, French, Prussians, a Russian prince with his own cook, and one American the description of whom I have been postponing, I notice, for as long as the story will let me, in the manner of a man postponing a smell.

I met him first as a voice, on the Monte Rosa veranda, the second evening of July. The voice was doing business at the far rail, in tones of molasses poured warm, and what it was saying was this:

"Madam, I would not sell it to you. Not to you. To a tourist, yes, tomorrow, and may his conscience fit him better than mine would fit me. But you, madam, a woman of feeling, who has stood where we stood today and felt what this valley holds in its stone heart, to you I could only ever *entrust* it."

The object being entrusted, into the gloved hands of a wide-eyed lady from, I later learned, Cleveland, was a shallow velvet case, and in the case, on white satin, lay three inches of old rope.

The man entrusting it wore a suit of pale gray, a planter's hat, and about forty extra years of the American South applied like varnish. He was sixty, built generous, barbered to a silver point, and he moved through the veranda crowd with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been punched and cannot imagine the arrangements. His card, which I would come to possess several of, against my will, like burrs, read:

COL. BYRON K. PREWITT
MEMPHIS · LUCERNE · THE WORLD
*Alpine Memorials & Historical Requisites*

The rope wants explaining, and the explanation is the man entire.

Every guest in Zermatt knew the story of 1865 before their trunks were unstrapped; it was the local wine, poured with everything. And the actual rope, the very manila that had parted on the north face with four men below it, existed. A length of it lay in a glass case in the Monte Rosa's little parlor cabinet, brown as a root, next to a barometer and a chamois horn, and guests filed past it after dinner and were quiet. There was no charge to look. The Seilers would as soon have charged admission to a grave.

Colonel Prewitt's contribution to the memorial economy was to sell the rope retail.

Fragments, madam. Recovered, madam, in circumstances he was not at liberty to fully disclose, from the effects of a guide's estate, authenticated by experts whose names were themselves entrusted only to purchasers. Each fragment in velvet, each with its certificate, ivory laid paper, a seal like a bishop's. Clemenz told me the guides' joke, which was that the Colonel had sold, by the village's arithmetic, some four hundred feet of a rope that had measured two hundred, and that if the sales continued the rope would soon be long enough to have saved everybody.

The joke stopped there, because the rest was not funny. The rest was the photographs.

I found them on the third of July, in the window of a shop by the church bridge that let its sill to the Colonel for the season: a dozen cabinet cards, *Memorial Portraiture by Appointment*, and I stood at that window perhaps ten minutes, and a passerby would have taken me for a man admiring. What I was doing was reading a fraud the way a locksmith reads a scratch on a lock.

Understand the goods. A widow, or a mother, or a fiancée, twenty or thirty years along in her grief, sits in a rented parlor in her best black. The Colonel's operator makes her portrait. When the print comes home to her hotel, there is a second figure in it, behind her chair, faint as breath on glass, a young man with an ice axe, his face just short of legible, his hand on, or nearly on, her shoulder. And under the mount, in copperplate: *He is nearer the summit now.*

Double exposure. The oldest trick in a trade that was born owning it, older than I was, exposed twice on one plate, the ghost figure a confederate posed against black velvet, underexposed to a whisper. Any operator alive could make one in an afternoon. That was not what held me at the window. What held me was the workmanship, which was terrible, and the clientele, which was not. The ghost's boots, in two of the cards, were the same boots at the same angle, because the Colonel's man was too idle to repose his confederate between victims. The ghost's face in a third had been scratched on the negative to blur it, the scratches plain as plow marks under a loupe. It was cheap work, hasty work, work that despised its buyer, and its buyers were women who had crossed an ocean to be nearer a grave that did not exist, because the ice had never given one up, and who would take that card home to Surrey or to Boston and put it on a table by the bed and believe, every remaining evening of their lives, that the mountain had let him lean down once and touch her.

I have thought a long time about why that window put such a coldness through me, when my own firm sold sentiment by the carton and I had posed my share of it. I believe it is this. A stereograph of the Rigi sunrise says: *here is a true thing you cannot come and see; hold it to the light and it will stand up honestly before you.* The Colonel's card said: *here is the one thing you want in the world, and it is a lie, and the lie is priced to what you have.* Both trades run on longing. One of them feeds it, the other farms it.

* * *

He introduced himself to me, of course. Men like Prewitt inventory a village inside a week, and I was the item that didn't match the shelf: an American operator, colored, alone, with a mule-load of first-class equipment and the Stroud name behind him. I was developing at the cellar door when the pale suit arrived in it, hat already off, hand already out, warmth already at the full, like a lamp carried into a smokehouse.

"Colonel Byron Prewitt. And you are the Stroud man, and I am honored, sir, honored beyond the ordinary run of the word." I will spare the reader most of the aria. It ran eight or ten minutes and touched upon the dignity of the photographic art, the vulgarity of the age, his admiration, expressed with a connoisseur's particularity, for the firm's Egyptian series, and his certainty, delivered with a bow of exactly calibrated depth, that a man of my *evident cultivation* had experienced, in our shared republic, treatment unworthy of him, a subject on which the Colonel wished me to know his own views were fifty years in advance of the common. This last was the piece I admired. A crude man flatters your work. Prewitt had sized me at a glance and flattered my wounds.

"And your predecessor, now," he said, at the door, hat resuming, timing rehearsed to read as afterthought. "Fell. There was an artist of the *genuine* stamp. We had dealings, small dealings, seasons past. Whatever has become of him? One hears he left effects."

"One hears more than I do, Colonel."

"No doubt, no doubt." He smiled with everything but the eyes, which stayed on me, taking their own kind of exposure. "Well. A great house looks after its property. Should you ever find the firm's interests and mine in, let us say, adjacency, my card."

He entrusted me with the card and went up the cellar stair, and paused at the top of it, because men like Prewitt exit on a line, like actors.

"This valley, Mr. Joiner," he said, gazing out at the street with real, unfeigned satisfaction, the satisfaction of a farmer surveying bottomland, "is the finest property in the memorial line on this continent. Thirty years of mourning, sir, with a railway to it."

* * *

That same week I saw the two ropes pass each other, the sold one and the true.

It was outside the guides' room, morning, the street full of departures. Prewitt had a client at the shop sill, a fresh widow of the ordinary kind, not a mountaineer's, just death's, being shown the velvet cases, and ten feet away, going to work with a coil over his shoulder and an Englishman's rucksack in his hand, passed a thickset man of about fifty-five with a beard going gray and a face that had been outdoors since the Flood, and Clemenz beside me took off his cap, not to be seen doing it, and put it back.

"Taugwalder," he said, when the man was past. "Peter. The son. He was on it."

"On the mountain? In sixty-five?"

"Twenty-two years old. On the rope, Herr Joiner. The one that held." Clemenz watched the broad back go up the street. "His father also. Old Peter. Him the English talked about after." He looked at his boots, and gave me two sentences of it, no more, in the voice of a man handing over something the village keeps wrapped. "They said the old man cut it. It is a lie, the inquiry said also it is a lie, but a lie with legs, this one. It walked him to America and back. He is dead now nine years."

Down the street, the Colonel's voice rose warm on the morning air. *To you, madam, I could only ever entrust it.*

Peter Taugwalder the younger went by the shop window without turning his head by one degree, which is its own kind of looking, and shouldered through the hotel door to collect his Englishman, and I stood in the July sunshine between the two of them, the man who carried the rope and the man who sold it, and that evening, for the first time since I had buckled it shut in June, I took Fell's box marked SPOILED down from the wardrobe, and set it on the table, and lit both lamps.
