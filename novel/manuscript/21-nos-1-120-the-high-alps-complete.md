# 21. Nos. 1–120: The High Alps, Complete

September came into the valley the way the tide goes out, all at once and nobody watching the moment it turned. The season's last great parties went up and came down; the cots left the reading rooms; the Gornergrat works paid off half their Italians, who went south singing on the Tuesday train like a freight of finches; and the light changed, the summer's white authority going amber at the edges, so that every operator in me itched for one more month of plates and every other part knew the year was folding its tripod.

The letter from Scotland came on the eighth, to Forsyth, with an enclosure for the chaplain and one line, forwarded under the solicitor's cover, for Fell. She read us the material paragraphs on the Riffelalp terrace at the corner table, in the flat voice, both hands on the paper against the wind.

The dowager marchioness thanked the daughter of Professor Forsyth for the survey record, which she had read, she wrote, entire, twice, with her spectacles and then with her glass, and found more comfort in than in most sermons, exactness being, at her age, the only tenderness left that did not condescend. The button she acknowledged in one sentence I will not copy out, because there is a species of sentence that should be read only by the person it was written to, and the rest of us that morning heard it once, which was already one hearing more than our right. And the business paragraph, the solicitor's paragraph, was this: that in memory of her son, and in recognition of a debt her family had owed since 1866 to a man it had never thanked, provision had been made, under terms of the strictest anonymity, for the continuance of the glacier survey of the late Professor David Forsyth, at his daughter's direction, for a term of not less than twenty years; the fund to be styled, in all documents, with no name in it at all, the Line Endowment.

Forsyth folded the letter along its creases. She looked down the valley for a while at the ice, her line, her father's line, twenty years of Augusts standing suddenly in a row in front of her, rungs in a ladder, and what she finally said, she said in exactly the voice she used for freight rates, which was how we knew to leave it alone for the present.

"He asked eleven pounds of the Alpine Club, over twenty-seven years. Eleven." She put the letter into the oilcloth satchel, with the tables. "Boy. The instrument. We have readings."

But that evening, the readings taken, she stood us all a dinner at the Riffelalp, the whole board of directors, Clemenz scrubbed to the ears and struck nearly dumb by the wine list, and at the end of it she rose and proposed the only toast I ever heard her make, six words, in the manner of a woman firing a survey shot straight over the head of thirty years of the Alpine Journal, and we drank it standing: "Gentlemen. The ghoulish arithmetic. It carried."

* * *

The portraits were her idea, which no one who knew her would believe, and I set it down here as evidence. It was the last week; she was closing the season's stations; and she remarked, at stake nine, with her back to me, in the tone of a woman auditing sundries, that her father had worked eleven summers on that moraine and there existed no photograph of him at the work, an omission she had thought about, she said, more than the omission deserved, and that if the view trade wished at last to make itself useful in the high country, the view trade was standing on it, with a camera.

So I made her portrait at stake nine, Alexandra Forsyth at the theodolite, bareheaded in the wind of the twentieth of September, one hand on the brass, her eye not on my lens, on her line, on the ice running away up-valley to the wall at the bottom of everything, and I made it in stereo, because depth was her subject before it was mine: in the viewer she stands off the gray world of the moraine the way the living stand off the past, and the instrument under her hand carries, and you cannot see it in the card but I will report it, a small brass plate on the tripod head, her father's, worn illegible everywhere but the two initials.

Then she photographed me. I want it understood what was being asked. I had stood behind a camera since boyhood precisely because the trade's whole bargain is that the operator is not in the view; I had been at ease on that side of the glass in three countries that had never once been easy about me; and no photograph of Hollis Joiner existed later than a Redlands studio card at age eleven, my mother's property, in which I wear a collar that still itches in memory. Forsyth took instruction on the focusing cloth and the plate holders with the grimness of a woman learning a rival's mathematics, ordered me onto the crest with the mountain over my shoulder, studied me upside down on the ground glass for a long moment, and then said, from under the cloth, the whole of her direction as a portraitist: "You're listening for the ice, Mr. Joiner. Go on listening."

The plate is before me now, its pair in the viewer's rack these forty years. A tall man in field clothes on a moraine crest, seventy pounds of another trade's luggage stacked by his boots, listening. She got the head, as the herdsman would have said. First exposure, no reposing. I have been photographed since by three studios and a newspaper, and they all got the hat.

We said goodbye on the Riffelalp path on the first of October, she going down to Visp on the morning train, Edinburgh, the school term, the winter of tables and correspondence. The correspondence part was settled by then, without either of us conducting a negotiation: she had the address of the firm and I had the address of the school, and the first letter, it was agreed as freight is agreed, would be hers, she having the season's data to summarize and I having, she observed, nothing to offer the exchange but California weather and opinions. She shook hands like a colleague, which from her it was, and had gone ten steps down the path when she stopped and turned around, into the sun, so that I never did see the expression that went with it, and paid me the only personal remark of the entire transaction, over her shoulder, in the voice of catechism:

"My father would have liked you, Mr. Joiner. He held that patience was the whole of character, and the rest ornament." A pause, the length of a survey sight. "He'd have liked Mr. Fell better. You'll neither of you mind; it was a small class of man he liked at all."

And she went down the path with the boy and the instrument behind her, and I stood in the larches listening to the season end, and minded not at all, and mind not yet.

* * *

The rest of the leavings were quickly made, as the true ones are.

Peter Taugwalder, met the last Sunday outside the church, did not shake my hand, but stood with me a moment in the thinning morning crowd and then said that the third table by the dining-room window had coffee at it, most fine mornings, at half past six, and that a man who wintered in the valley, or came back to it, would be expected, and went in to Mass, having conferred, I was given to understand by Herr Imboden's face across the street, something the house had watched him confer twice before in thirty years.

Clemenz Biner received: the Geneva man's camera, bought fair from the firm's petty account and entered as equipment, western department, on permanent field deployment; my cellar stock of unexposed plates; a winter's standing commission, one plate a month from the masonry station, first light, the station notebook to be kept in English, *for the practice*; and wages for it that Stroud will discover in the ledgers on the day the recording angel audits us all. He walked me to the train on the last morning wearing the black hat, which had never gone back to its owner and never would, the owner having pronounced it *deployed for scale*, and he did not manage any of the three farewell speeches he had plainly composed, and instead handed my cases up the way he had carried them all season, as though the whole freight of the summer were inside, which it was, and said, at the window, with the hat over his heart, "Herr Joiner. The season was — " and there the great hotel vocabulary, four winters of Territet, the finest English in the village, failed entire, and nineteen years old looked up out of it, "— it was *very superior*," and could not go on, and did not need to.

Fell came to the station too. He was for the south the following week, Italy, an appointment by All Saints, he said, of which no more, and after that, when I asked it straight, he looked down the platform at the mountain a while and said that a man who had spent thirty years photographing appointments other people kept had a few of his own now in arrears, and the trade would hear of him or it wouldn't, and either way the plates would exist, which was the only clause that had ever mattered. He handed me a parcel wrapped in the hotel's own paper. I knew the weight of it before the string was off, having carried its brothers all summer: the station notebook, 1878 to 1897, the whole High Alps in the narrow upright hand, times, stones, weathers, the twenty minutes that were the picture, every key to every lock in that country.

"The list's yours now," he said. "Both lists. The firm's, and the other one, the one you'll start keeping without noticing, of the subjects that aren't on the firm's. Everybody in this trade worth their silver keeps the second list. The difference between a hack and an operator, Mr. Joiner, is which list he dies furthest down." Then the guard's whistle went, and Ambrose Fell shook my hand once, hard, and stood back, and offered as his whole valediction, deadpan, in the voice of a man returning a borrowed tool: "Change your wash water more. You're one habit from first class."

The train went down the valley in the October light. I did look back, once, past Randa, where the great tusk of it swings clear of the shoulder for a last mile, and I made no ceremony of the looking and the mountain made none of being looked at, standing there in its early snow with its cloud going out to the east like smoke from a good chimney, four thousand feet of it over the graves of its own churchyard, keeping, under the new white, the rest of whatever it keeps.
