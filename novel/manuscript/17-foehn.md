# 17. Föhn

What does merchandise want. I never answered that wire, and it has waited patiently in the notebook forty years, so let it be answered here: in the twelve days that followed, the merchandise and I finished the catalogue together, and if the reader has gathered by now that I would not trade those twelve days for the firm entire, the reader has been attending.

We worked the remaining numbers as a two-man crew, Fell keeping always above the village line, myself fronting the operation to Zermatt, and the division suited the list, whose stubbornest outstanding subjects were the high stations, the cols and the cabins, the places wanting legs and settled weather and a second pair of hands worth having. I carried; he chose the minute. I set the camera to the old station notes; he stood behind the ground glass making the sound in his throat, a single low note like a door easing, that I learned inside two mornings meant *the note was right in '78 and the world has moved an inch since; move it back.* He gave instruction the way the better sort of master does, by letting you watch the difference your obedience made, and only once in the twelve days did he deliver anything you could bottle as doctrine. It was on the Riffel, waiting out a cloud with the plate loaded, when I asked him how he had known, at the Rigi twenty years before, to rate the crowd the subject and the sunrise the lamp. He thought about it the whole life of that cloud, eleven minutes, and then said: "Everything in front of a lens is either weather or witness. Sort them right and the exposure sorts itself," and then, before the sentence could settle its collar and become philosophy, "Cloud's thinning. Wet the plate."

Number 87 we made on the seventeenth of August, the last number of the re-made set, kept last on purpose by both of us without either confessing to the arranging of it. The Matterhorn from the east, morning, the old station on the Gorner moraine, the boot-worn flats where a tripod had stood in 1878, and where the black-hatted guide of my mother's parlor had waited the size of a beetle in creation. Fell would not stand in. "I was the hat man's employer," he said. "The place is filled by rank." So it was Clemenz Biner who walked out onto the moraine in Fell's own black hat, borrowed for the exposure and two sizes shy, and stood where the ice would read as ice and the mountain would leap back its four miles, nineteen years old, upright as a fencepost, prouder than princes, and that is the figure in the card, if you own the set the firm sold for the next forty years, and now you know his name and what he charged for the sitting, which was the hat.

I capped the lens on the second exposure and stood up from the hood, and the two of us looked at each other over the camera in the morning wind, on the spot where his life's work and my life's direction had crossed nineteen years before either of us knew the other breathed, and neither of us said one word about it, and that silence was the handsomest payment I ever took in the trade.

The list was whole. One hundred and twenty numbers, sixty-one of his season, fifty-nine of mine, though I defy any stranger to sort every plate to its right column, and the freight went down to Basle under seal on the eighteenth, and I wired Chicago: CATALOGUE COMPLETE ALL NUMBERS FIRST QUALITY. And that same evening the weather turned its coat.

* * *

The Edinburgh answer had come on the fourteenth, to the Riffelalp, registered, and Forsyth had brought it to the hut herself, unopened, and read it to us standing, by the door, in the flat voice she kept for things that would not bear expression added.

It was from the family's man of business, four paragraphs, and it said: that her communication had been received and laid, with all delicacy, before the person chiefly concerned, the mother of the lost gentleman, now very aged; that the family desired no announcement, no attendance of its own, and no correspondence with any public print, the family having had, the solicitor permitted himself, sufficient acquaintance with the newspapers in recent years to last several generations; that should Providence, through the agency her late father had foretold, restore what the mountain kept, the family's whole instruction was this, and the solicitor begged it be followed to the letter: identification to be established privately and certainly; the remains to be laid beside his companions of that day, in Zermatt, without name upon the stone if a stone there must be, the register to record what the clergyman's conscience required and no more; and word, one private word only, to be sent when it was done.

The fourth paragraph was to Forsyth herself, and she read it more slowly. The dowager marchioness wished it conveyed that she had, in the year 1866, read Professor Forsyth's letter to the Journal; that she had never joined in the laughter; that she had, on the contrary, kept the cutting; and that if the professor's daughter now stood at the foot of that glacier in his stead, she asked of her one kindness beyond all the science, that whatever the ice restored, however small, some little thing of it, a button, a thread, might be sent north, privately, to a mother, before the earth closed over the rest.

Forsyth folded the letter along its creases and put it in the oilcloth satchel with her father's tables, which I judged then and judge now to have been the correct filing, and none of the three of us was fit for talk for a while, and she took the readings at six sharp regardless, because the readings were at six.

* * *

The föhn announced itself first in the barometer, then in the animals, then in everything. Herr Imboden tapped the glass at noon on the eighteenth and looked at it the way you look at a debtor's note of hand. By four the sky had gone to a high milky scud with the summits standing in it queerly close and hard-edged, the whole range come forward like the near plane of a badly mounted card. The cows came down off the pastures of their own opinion. The air arrived warm, then warmer, in long dry pushes off the col, smelling of stone, and the old men of the village sat out on the benches sniffing it and naming it to each other with the grim satisfaction of prophets on schedule.

It blew for a night and a day and a second night, and I will not decorate it: a föhn is not a storm out of scripture, only a warm wind with no conscience whatever about snow. It ate the fresh August powder off the whole east face in the first twelve hours and went to work on firn that had thought itself pensioned. The village shutters banged themselves hoarse. The Gornergrat works shut down when a crane went over. Every gutter, every runnel, every stone channel cut by four hundred years of melt ran loud and brown, and under the wind, all the second night, you could hear the valley's true business going forward in the dark: water, more water than the maps allowed for, hurrying down out of ten glaciers toward the young Rhône with the year's whole ration paid out in a night.

Forsyth appeared at the Monte Rosa at first light on the twentieth with the boy asleep on his feet beside her, having been up at her stakes in the tail of the wind, which I forbore to remark on and she forbore to defend.

"Nineteen inches on the mid-line since Thursday," she said. "The tongue has come on more in two days than the first fortnight of July entire. And the arch is down. The south half of the cave mouth collapsed in the night; the river's cutting the north wall as we stand here." She accepted coffee the way the exhausted do, as cargo. "The wall it is cutting, Mr. Joiner, is the wall."

Clemenz came in an hour behind her, down from Staffel at a pace that had opinions of its own, with a note from Fell in the narrow upright hand, and the note said what the note had waited thirty-one years, a stone station, and one warm wind to say, and said it in the only dialect the man had ever fully trusted, which was the shot list's:

*Subject at hand. Light wants two days to settle and so does the ice; the wall is shedding. Bring on the 22nd, at first light: Taugwalder, the English chaplain, the lady, the boy if she vouches him, yourself. Litter, sound rope, two lanterns trimmed short. No other soul. Wear your church boots under your work boots. — A.F.*

There was a postscript, and the postscript was the man entire, and I have never decided whether it belongs to the comedy of this account or to the other column, and after forty years I let it stand in both:

*P.S. The firm's seal on the Basle freight was well struck. You wax a seal like a joiner. Your father's trade? Bring nothing of the firm's on the 22nd, but bring the habit.*

The twenty-first we spent on the quiet arrangements such a matter wants, and they are quieter than the reader may suppose, there being a whole grammar of discretion in a village with the scar. The chaplain, informed in the vestry after Evensong, asked one question only, whether the family had been consulted, and being shown the solicitor's paragraph, bowed his head a moment and then went and stood a long time at his shelf deciding which order of burial a man carries to a glacier. Herr Imboden was told nothing and arranged everything, a mule ordered for half past three, a hamper, the yard gate oiled, in the manner of a man conspicuously not asking where his oldest guest's chair had got to. And Peter Taugwalder the younger, met at his own door in the dusk with his supper still on the table behind him, listened to me say it, the ice, the wall, the morning after next, his own two hands wanted at the bottom of the mountain his father never came down from in the village's eyes, and stood in the doorway a while with the lamplight on one side of his face.

"The twenty-second," he said at last. "It is a Sunday."

"The chaplain knows."

"Good," said Taugwalder. "My father was religious." And he went in to his supper, and I walked back up the emptied street to the hotel, and lay a long time listening to the milk-brown water running under everything, and did not trim the lanterns that night, because Fell had said short and my hands wanted daylight for it, and there are tasks a man saves deliberately for the eve of a thing, to have somewhere to put his hands, and that one was mine, and I record that at the last, past midnight, the water's sound came apart the way rain does when you fall asleep in it, into syllables, none of them words.
