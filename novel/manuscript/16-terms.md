# 16. Terms

Alexandra Forsyth climbed to Staffelalp two mornings later with her boy, her theodolite, and her father's tables in the oilcloth satchel, having replied to my note of invitation with a note of correction, that she had been intending to call on Mr. Fell's station in any case, the masonry being on her moraine.

I have attended, in the years since, perhaps a hundred meetings of consequence, in board rooms with water glasses, and not one of them was chaired as well as the meeting held that day on a plank bench against a cheese hut wall at seven thousand feet. It opened with the apology, which Fell delivered as promised, standing, hat in hand, in four sentences he had evidently carpentered over a year and sanded to the grain: that he had come to her survey a stranger with a commercial eye and given her every reason to price him accordingly; that her price had been correct at the time it was set; that he had spent the year since attempting to become the man who should have stood on that moraine; and that her father's letter of 1866 had been right in every clause, including the ones the Journal mocked, and he would say so to any body of men she cared to name, on any continent, under oath if wanted.

Forsyth heard it through with her chin up and her hands folded on the satchel, in the posture I imagine she took for school inspections, and when he had done she looked at him for a moment with a terrifying brightness about the eyes, and then said, in her levelest voice, the voice of catechism and freight rates:

"My father held, Mr. Fell, that an error corrected is data, and a man corrected is rarer and better. Sit down. You've been standing thirty-one years by my reckoning, and we have a fortnight, and I want to see the plates from this near line of yours, because if your method and my line agree, we will know the hour of this thing to a morning, and if they disagree, one of us is about to learn something."

They agreed. I will spare the reader the working, which took the day, the boy running sights, Fell printing from his little tent of a field darkroom, myself between them as translator, for they came at every quantity from opposite ends, she from rate and section, he from parallax and plate, and each checked the other's tongue the way emigrants check a contract in two languages. By four in the afternoon the sums lay side by side on the bench under stones, and they closed like a bracket: her envelopes, his fusions, the cave mouth, the constellation in the wall, and the answer, weather permitting or weather hastening, was the third week of August, and the place was the arch where the river came out, and the reader knows all this, but I stood over that bench a long moment regardless, because it is one thing to hold a conviction and another to watch two independent instruments arrive at it from different sides of thirty years, and set it down between them in pencil, twice.

"Then we are three," said Forsyth, folding her sheets in their order, "plus the boy, plus your Clemenz, plus whatever answer is riding up the valley from Edinburgh, and one too many Colonels. Which brings us to the ugly item on the agenda, gentlemen, and I move we take it standing. I don't sit for vermin."

* * *

The Colonel had made his opening the evening before, as it happened, on the Monte Rosa veranda, in the manner of a man who had decided that the season of hints was closed and the season of terms open.

He had drawn a chair to my table uninvited, ordered two cognacs with a benevolence that assumed the second, and laid it out for me, gently, collegially, the pale eyes doing their own arithmetic all the while: that he was an old man in a young man's hurry; that the wide world held perhaps five commercial situations in a century worth what this valley was about to be worth, and he had smelled every one of the five and arrived late to four; that eleven plates of one piece of ice, a Scotch lady with survey stakes, and the vanishing of the one operator in Europe with the patience of God all summed, for a man who could add, to a single famous name, and to what he called, leaning in, lowering the molasses to a reverence, *the recovery of the age.* And he proposed a syndicate. My optics, the lady's science, Fell's plates if living or legend if dead, and the Colonel's, he said it without one grain of shame, *market*: the exclusive photographs, the illustrated weeklies, the lecture circuit, the memorial cabinet cards, the authenticated relics, sir, conceive of it, no more three-inch ropes, the genuine article at last, *provenanced*, and the mourning of two nations thirty-two years banked and drawing interest, ready to be called in a single season by the men holding the paper.

I said no with the cognac untouched, in the fewest words I could build the wall of, and the Colonel sat back and regarded me a while, and the warmth went out of the performance the way heat goes out of a stone after dark, gradually, and with the shape of the stone unchanged.

"Well," he said at last. "You're Fell's man now. I'd heard it and I didn't credit it; a firm's man twice over, I said, that one, wages from Chicago and worship up the mountain, no room at either trough for old Prewitt." He stood, and settled the planter's hat, and delivered the rest down at me in a voice I had not heard from him before, the patter gone, the drawl gone dry and level, the voice, I supposed, that Memphis had heard across card tables before the war made colonels of such men. "Then hear the alternative, son, since partnership's declined. I don't need your ice, or your lady's chalk marks, or the hour and the spot. I need a question and a wire office. LOST LORD OF THE MATTERHORN, GLACIER GIVING UP ITS DEAD, question mark. The London press pays for questions, Mr. Joiner, answers are for scholars. I send that wire, and inside six days this valley has forty correspondents, four hundred trippers, a photographer behind every stone of that moraine, and a syndicate anyway, only a worse one, with no science in it and no manners, trampling your lady's stakes and dynamiting the snout for souvenirs, and whatever that ice gives up, it gives up in front of the crowd. That's the other shelf, and you'll have put me to it." He set two fingers to the hat brim. "Talk to your principals. The offer closes Saturday."

* * *

"He means it," said Fell, when I had rendered the veranda scene entire, there against the hut wall. "It's how he took the Tell chapel trade in '81, and the Lucerne lion before that. When the goods won't come to the Colonel, the Colonel raises a crowd, and the crowd brings the goods." He looked at Forsyth. "Six days of correspondents against a fortnight of ice. He can beat us to our own appointment without leaving the telegraph office."

"Then he must be given something better to do with Saturday," said Forsyth, "or something worse to fear than missing it," and it says a great deal about the composition of our board that all three of us arrived at the shape of the answer inside the same silence, and that the first to say a word of it aloud was the one of us raised strictest, who put down her pencil and remarked, to the middle distance, in the tone of a woman consenting at last to serve sherry on the Sabbath:

"He sells false relics, gentlemen. It seems to me the market in false relics wants regulating."

The mechanics we built that afternoon, and I set them down without apology, since the reader by now owns every part needed to assemble them. Item: one belt buckle of heavy silver plate, purchased plain in Brig by Clemenz the following Tuesday. Item: the skill of Ambrose Fell, who had aged photographic props for backgrounds since before I was born, and who took to the work of thirty years' burial in an afternoon of acids, ash, and glacier flour, with a workmanship so loving that Forsyth inspected the result under her lens and pronounced it dreadful, meaning perfect. Item: engraved upon the back, by my own hand and Fell's letter-forms, a monogram, F.W.B.D., correct to the young lord's names, which any Alpine history in any hotel reading room would supply. Item, and this was Fell's clause, the trap's pin, laid in with the finality of a man who had dealt with appraisers his whole life: on the buckle's underside, small, half worn, honest as sin, a Birmingham hallmark, with the date letter for 1871.

Six years after the mountain took its dead.

"Any assayer, any auction house, any museum man in London reads that mark in one minute with a glass," said Fell, turning the little slander of silver in the lamplight. "No expert alive misses it, and no glutton in a hurry looks. If the Colonel buys quietly and sells quietly, his own goods hang him the first time a buyer's man of business gets curious. If he goes to his newspapers, he goes holding merchandise that shouts fraud at the first touch of daylight. Either way, the day the ice renders its true account, Byron Prewitt will be the one man on this continent who cannot afford to be discovered within a mile of it, holding what he holds." He wrapped the buckle in cheesecloth like a pastry. "The shepherd found it in the outwash below the snout, gravel in the keeper, and wants silver and silence. Clemenz's cousin has the face for it. Let the Colonel beat him down on the price. He'll believe anything he's cheated a poor man for."

"It is a lie," said Forsyth, to no one of us in particular, testing the plank of it with her whole Presbyterian weight before trusting it. "We are answering a grave-robber with forgery."

"It is a lie with a confession sewn into its lining, Miss Forsyth, legible to any honest eye that ever examines it," said Fell. "That is as near the truth as anything the Colonel has touched in thirty years. And it does not rob a grave. It builds a fence around one."

She considered, in her audit silence, the pencil upright under her finger like a plumb line.

"The eighteen seventy-one letter," she said at last. "You're certain of the cycle? Birmingham, not London?"

"Madam," said Fell, with the second smile I ever saw from him, "I have forged more hallmarks for stage properties than the assay office ever struck. Trust the craft, if you won't trust the craftsman."

"I find," said Alexandra Forsyth, gathering her satchel, entirely straight-faced, in the voice of catechism, "that I am coming to trust both, which my minister shall hear about at a date of my choosing. Good evening, gentlemen. Boy, the instrument. We have readings at six."

* * *

Saturday morning, from the post office, I wired Chicago:

MERCHANDISE LOCATED SOUND STOP RECOVERY IN NEGOTIATION REQUIRES PATIENCE AND NO INQUIRIES REPEAT NO INQUIRIES STOP CATALOGUE WILL COMPLETE STOP JOINER

The reply came Monday, and I have kept it flat in the same notebook as the sleeve inscription and the ruled margin, the three papers of that summer I would run into a burning house for:

PATIENCE GRANTED ONE FORTNIGHT STOP WHAT DOES MERCHANDISE WANT STOP EVERY MAN WANTS SOMETHING FIND OUT WIRE COST STROUD

And on the Saturday evening, as the offer closed, Colonel Byron K. Prewitt, in the snuggery of a pension at the lower end of the village, having sworn a ragged shepherd cousin to secrecy on a saint the Colonel could not have named, bought for eleven francs, beaten down with real cruelty from thirty, the recovered belt buckle of Lord Francis William Bouverie Douglas, and locked it in his dispatch case, and stood the room a round of Fendant, and did not wire London at all.
