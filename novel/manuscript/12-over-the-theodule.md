# 12. Over the Théodule

The parcel record was the postmaster's to give or withhold, and he withheld it for nine days, on principle, the principle being that a Swiss official is not an almanac to be consulted by every passing firm. What moved him in the end was not my letter of authority. It was Herr Imboden, who walked across the street with me on the tenth morning and said three sentences to him in dialect, of which I understood one word, *Fell*, and the postmaster looked at me over his spectacles for a while, and then opened his ledger of forwardings as though opening the parish register of souls.

One entry. November the 19th, 1896. A parcel of books, forwarded at the addressee's standing instruction to: *A. Fell, presso Pensione Ghiglione, Vernazza, Liguria, Regno d'Italia.*

"Liguria," said Clemenz, reading over my shoulder, in the tone of a man confirming a long-held suspicion about foreigners. "The sea." He gave it the pronunciation mountain people keep for the sea: a rumor's.

I wired Stroud that the Italian numbers on the shot list would be made that fortnight, which was true, and that I was pursuing the firm's merchandise, which was also true, and I have noticed all my life that the most useful truths come in pairs like that, each holding the other's coat. Three numbers of the old *High Alps* had been made from the south side of the mountain: the Matterhorn from Breuil, where it stands up as a different mountain entirely, a horn of Africa, Fell had written in his station notes, *grown in Italy out of Italian rock and slandered by the Swiss profile*; the Théodule pass itself; and the towers of the Valtournenche. The direct road to all three, and to Liguria beyond, went over the glacier at eleven thousand feet, and the road had been in use, Clemenz assured me, since the Romans, whom he cited as other men cite their grandfathers.

We crossed on the last Tuesday of July: myself, Clemenz, and a Valtournenche man named Fabien picked up at the Gandegg hut, because custom and sense both said the glacier wanted three on the rope. We left at two in the morning under a moon you could have read contracts by.

I had been two months photographing glaciers. This was my first morning standing on one, and the difference is the difference between the gallery and the cage. The Théodule at that hour was a plain of gray iron, tilted, endless, creaking. Underfoot it was not silent and it was not loud; it ticked, and now and then, somewhere under the crust, at a depth the mind declined to price, something shifted its shoulder and the whole plain said a word in a language older than water. The rope ran moon-white from my waist forward to Fabien and back to Clemenz, and I understood inside the first mile why the men of this trade speak of the rope the way sailors speak of the ship, as the actual party, of which the persons attached are members.

Near the top of the pass, in the blue hour, Fabien stopped us with one lifted hand, prodded the snow ahead with his axe, twice, gently, the way you wake a sleeper you respect, and a yard of the road in front of him sighed and went down without haste into a blackness that did not report a bottom. We stood, the three of us, breathing, and looked into it, and the cold that came up out of that slot was not the morning's cold. It was the building's cold, the cellar-breath of the whole structure, and I thought, with a clarity I have never managed since at sea level: this is what Forsyth's tables measure and my plates portray, this black patience, and both of us are courtiers describing a king from the anteroom, and here is the audience chamber door, open a yard.

Fabien found the road's better opinion twenty feet to the west, and we went on, and at half past six we stood on the frontier, a ridge of wind-raked rock with Italy falling away below in a hundred miles of blue and bronze, and Clemenz, who had made the crossing before, produced from his rucksack, with the timing of a great head waiter, three ends of bread and a tin of anchovies, *the specialty of the house*.

The Matterhorn from the Théodule at seven in the morning was Number 96 of the new series, and I made it with the wind trying my patience and the tripod's, and it is as good as anything I have signed my life to, and the firm sold it for forty years, and not one purchaser in ten thousand can have known that the operator's hands were still remembering, through the whole exposure, a yard of road going down without haste into the dark.

* * *

Breuil in 1897 was six buildings and a chapel at the top of a green wilderness, with the south face of the mountain standing over it like a proclamation. Old Élie Pession kept the inn, and mule traffic, and the passing history of the valley, and when he understood, over soup, whose trail I walked, he sent his granddaughter for a better bottle and settled in to render accounts.

Yes. The English signore of the photographs. Late in September, last year, over from Zermatt with two cases and the black mule he had hired eleven summers running, always the same mule, a beast of prejudices, and the signore its one exception. He had lodged four nights, paid in silver, photographed nothing, which Pession had found notable, a man carrying a church organ across the Alps and declining to play it. He had sat evenings on the bench there, by the door, looking up at the mountain.

"Ours is the true face of it," said Pession, through Fabien's French and my own, the granddaughter correcting both. "The Swiss have the profile, we have the face. The signore said to me: from Breuil the mountain looks like what it is. I asked him: and what is it? And he thought a long time, this man, a serious man, and he said: *a creditor.*" Pession spread his hands. "Then in the morning he went down the valley with the carrier to Châtillon, for the railway, and the mule went home over the pass with my nephew, and that is all of it, except that in the spring, in May, he came back through, going up, with provisions for a long stay bought in Châtillon, and he did not lodge, and he did not sit, and when my granddaughter asked him would he take soup, he thanked her like a bishop and said he was expected." He refilled my glass. "Expected. I have thought about the word. There is nothing up that road, signore, but the pass, and after the pass, Zermatt. And no one in Zermatt, by your own account, has seen him."

"There is one thing up that road," said Fabien slowly, "besides the pass."

Pession looked at him, and something passed between the two Valtournenche men at the pace of local knowledge, and Fabien turned to me. "The high pastures on the Swiss side. Above the Zmutt. A man who leaves the mule road below the Gandegg and goes north, over the shoulder, comes down to the alps at Staffel and Schönbiel with no one in Zermatt the wiser. It is not a tourist's road. It is a smuggler's road, and a shepherd's."

"And a photographer's," I said, "if the photographer had a reason."

Nobody at the table asked what the reason might be, Pession because he was an innkeeper, Fabien because he was on wages, and Clemenz because he had been beside me on the moraine when Fell's three stones came out from under the gravel, and had the whole ledger added long since. He only looked at me across the lamp with his eyebrows at half staff, a man declining to say *I have told you the ice keeps books* in front of strangers.

* * *

We made the two remaining Italian numbers the next day, and the day after that came down the Valtournenche on the carrier's cart through a morning of hayfields and church bells, every village smaller and warmer, the mountain shutting itself away behind shoulder after green shoulder until it was gone entire, which no one had warned me a mountain could be, and by evening we were at Châtillon on the floor of the great valley of Aosta, in vineyards, in summer, in Italy, and the train came in from Turin smelling of coal and peaches.

I sent Clemenz home over the pass with Fabien and the equipment, all but one case, there being fifty-nine reasons of the firm's for the equipment not to holiday at the seaside, and Clemenz took his wages and his orders, the orders being to keep the catalogue's appearance of progress and his own counsel, and to learn, by the harmless means at which the village excelled, who was buying milk and cheese this summer at the alps above the Zmutt, and to have the answer by my return.

"And if it is him?" said Clemenz, at the carriage window.

"Then nothing. Say nothing, do nothing, and above all photograph nothing. Just know where he sleeps."

The train took me down the Aosta valley in the last of the light, and at Turin I changed for the coast in the dark, and slept sitting up through Alessandria and woke somewhere south of Genoa because the air through the ventilator had changed its whole doctrine, salt and pine tar and heat that did not leave with the sun, and when I put up the blind the Mediterranean was running along below the track in the moonlight, close enough to hit with a cap, patient as ice and quicker about it, and I sat with my forehead on the cold glass like a boy, sixty hours from a glacier, watching my third sea.
