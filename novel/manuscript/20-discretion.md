# 20. Discretion

He was buried on the Monday, at night, which sounds furtive and was the opposite: it was the only hour the village could attend without the season attending too. Word had gone through Zermatt's own channels, below the hotel floors, in the kitchens and the guides' room and the stables, that the thing would be at eleven, and that it was the village's and no one else's, and I never learned that a single soul of the four thousand visitors then sleeping above those kitchens ever knew. The English churchyard at eleven held the seven of us, the chaplain now properly in his surplice, Herr Imboden with his staff of six standing behind him like a family, the pastor of the Swiss church, come in his cassock to stand at his colleague's shoulder on the theory, which he stated at the gate in French and did not argue, that a valley has one register in heaven whatever it keeps on earth, and then, coming quietly through the gate in twos and threes until they stood three deep along the wall, the guides. The black coats. I counted upward of forty, which was every man of the company then in the valley and some down from the huts who cannot have heard before eight o'clock, and not one of them had been sent for. They stood along the wall with their hats off and their ropes coiled on their shoulders, in the full dress of their trade, as they stand for one of their own, and Peter Taugwalder stood at the head of the grave, and his son, who was twelve, held the lantern.

The grave was dug beside his companions of that day. The chaplain read the order of burial entire, hurrying nothing, and where the office says the name he said only, in a steady voice, "our brother, whom God has known these two and thirty years by name," and the mountain stood over the churchyard in the starlight, enormous, half the sky's stars swallowed by the black of it, and when the first earth went down the guides along the wall did the thing I have never told without stopping a moment first. They rang their axes. Each man as the earth fell struck his ice axe head once, softly, against the churchyard wall's stone coping, one after another down the line, forty small clear strokes like a peal rung handwise, the sound their trade makes on a thousand mornings on the ice, going up; and that was the passing bell they gave him, the noise of the craft that had taken him and kept him and carried him down, and then it was quiet, and the earth went on falling, and that was all.

The rope went into the grave with him. That was settled the evening before, in the vestry, in a conference of ten minutes I was called into as a witness of the recovery, and it was Taugwalder's word that settled it. The chaplain had raised, with delicacy, the question of the knots: whether the rope ought not to be kept, deposited, attested, it being, he said, evidence, and the answer of Peter Taugwalder, delivered after his customary silence, in his deliberate English, was this: "Evidence is for a trial. There is no trial. There was never a trial, only talk. You cannot bury talk, Herr Kaplan, I have tried thirty years. But a rope you can bury." And then, laying his hand flat on the vestry table: "It held him twice about. Let it keep doing its work."

What was kept was paper. The chaplain drew up that night, and we signed before midnight, all seven, an attestation of what the ice had rendered and the condition of every article of it, the two turns, the doubled knot, the drawn burst of the parting, each particular set out in the dry actuarial English such documents want, and the document was sealed with the solicitor's letter into the parish safe, to lie there against the day, if it ever dawns, that print decides to retract a whisper. It lies there now. And the register of the English church at Zermatt carries, under the twenty-third of August, 1897, four words of Latin in the chaplain's small strong hand, and I copy them here as the whole public monument of that summer, and as sufficient:

*Advena, monti redditus. Deo notus.*

A stranger, given back by the mountain. Known to God.

As for the village, no proclamation was ever made, none being the village's way. What happened instead, Clemenz reported to me, was one sentence in the guides' room on the Tuesday morning, when the talk paused on some ordinary matter of tariffs, and old Perren of the committee looked across at Taugwalder and said, in dialect, gruffly, as such men pay thirty years of debt, "Your father tied well, Peter," and the room said nothing at all, in the way a room says amen, and the talk went on to tariffs, and that was the end of the slander of Peter Taugwalder the elder in the one jurisdiction where it had ever really been tried.

* * *

The parcel for Scotland went north on the Thursday, registered, from the post office of the postmaster who was not an almanac, and I will inventory it, having packed it myself in the cellar with the care I once kept for glass.

Item. A button of horn, from the waistcoat, cleaned of the ice and of nothing else.

Item. A certified extract of the register, in the chaplain's hand, with his seal.

Item. A copy, made fair by her own pen, of the page of survey record for the twenty-second of August, showing the place of rendering to lie within the window computed by Professor David Forsyth in the year 1866, together with, at her father's daughter's insistence, and I watched her rule it, the note: *Observed emergence within predicted limits. The prediction was his. The waiting was many people's. The kindness at the end was a whole village's.*

Item. One photograph, whole plate, printed by Ambrose Fell in my cellar over two nights of work that I was permitted to attend and not to assist: the bearing party small on the gray page of the moraine, walking down into the morning, the wall of ice above them gone to daylight, the mountain over all. He printed it as I have never seen printing done, the blacks kept breathing, the great distances open, the little company of figures dark and particular and unmistakably persons, each one, at a hundred feet, themselves; and when the second night's print came up in the dish to his satisfaction he washed it, and dried it between blotters like a pressed flower, and then in the corner, small as a grain of rice, in pencil, he signed it. I said nothing, but he heard me think it, the way he heard everything in a darkroom.

"It's not for sale," he said, "so it's mine to sign. That was always the arithmetic, Mr. Joiner. Sign nothing you'd sell; sell nothing you'd sign. I only ever got the one clause of it right at a time."

The negative he wrapped in three papers, and boxed, and it went into the parcel beside the print, and I asked him, because someone was going to, whether he kept no copy for himself.

"I was there," said Fell.

Chicago remains to be accounted, and the accounting ran by wire over four days, and I give the traffic as it stands pasted in my book, because no narration improves a Stroud telegram.

Mine, the Wednesday: CATALOGUE COMPLETE AND SHIPPED AS ADVISED STOP MERCHANDISE RECOVERED IN GOOD ORDER ASKS NOTHING REPEAT NOTHING STOP LETTER FOLLOWS EXPLAINING WHAT FIRM MAY NOT PRINT STOP JOINER

His, the Thursday: NOTHING IS NOT AN ANSWER EVERY MAN WANTS SOMETHING STOP WHAT DO YOU WANT STROUD

I walked around the village for an hour with that form in my pocket, and I composed, I suppose, thirty replies, and some of them asked for money, and some for the western department, and one, which lasted most of the hour, asked for nothing at all in a fine hard proud style that would have warmed me for a week and cost the trade the only lever it was ever going to get. In the end I sat down in the writing room among the season's honeymooners and sent the one my mother's son was always going to send, and I put my own name at the risk in it, which is the only place a name does any work:

NEW SET IS THE FINEST PROPERTY IN THE TRADE AND TRADE WILL KNOW INSIDE A YEAR STOP RIVAL HOUSES NOW PRINT ARTISTS NAMES AND TAKE OUR CUSTOM WITH THEM STOP PROPOSE BOX AND CATALOGUE CARRY LINE PHOTOGRAPHED BY A FELL AND H JOINER STOP COSTS NOTHING SELLS CARDS STOP IF IT FAILS DOCK MY WAGES FOR THE PRINTING STOP JOINER

The answer took two days, which for E. O. Stroud was a fortnight of the soul, and when it came it was, in its entirety, and I have it yet:

TRIED IT ON THE SALES MANAGER HE SAYS NAMES SELL STOP YOUR WAGES ARE SAFE WHICH GRIEVES ME STOP LINE WILL READ SUN SCULPTURE BY A FELL AND H JOINER STOP FELL FIRST HE IS OLDER STOP COME HOME VIA CHICAGO THERE IS A WESTERN DEPARTMENT TO DISCUSS STROUD

I carried it up to Staffelalp that evening, the last evening the hut was inhabited, and Fell read it twice by the door in the long light, and handed it back, and was quiet a while, looking down the valley at the ice, and I did not suppose the quiet was about the sales manager.

"Twenty-seven years ago," he said at last, "I'd have built that telegram a frame." He knocked out his pipe and stood, and gathered the last of his kit off the bench, and at the door of the hut he stopped and looked back down the moraine, one look the length of a season, or of several, and then said, in the voice he used for shot lists, "Well. Sun sculpture, by A. Fell. He's put me first for age and the compositor will think it's for rank. Let him think it, Mr. Joiner. Come down. The chair's waited long enough."

And Ambrose Fell shouldered his camera and walked down the west path to the village and slept that night, for the first time in fourteen months, in his own room above the bakery smell and the church bells, third floor south, where the bill had been rendered to Chicago because firms require bills, and the chair had waited, because that house did not sell a man's chair while he was on the mountain.
