# 11. The Weak Rope

The rain held Zermatt indoors for three days, and a resort indoors is a pressure vessel, and the Monte Rosa managed the pressure the way the house had managed everything for sixty years, with soup, whist, and rationed anecdote. On the second evening, when the parlor had exhausted the day's newspapers and a Manchester cotton man had twice proposed charades, Herr Imboden crossed to the little cabinet between the barometer and the chamois horn, unlocked it with a key from his own ring, and lifted out the glass case with the rope in it, and the room went quiet before he had turned around.

He did this, I came to learn, perhaps twice a season, by a private almanac of his own: when the weather was foul, when the company was decent, and when some regulating instinct told him the story was about to be told anyway, badly, by somebody else, and had better be told right.

I will give it as he gave it, shorn of my questions and the cotton man's, because Imboden's version was the valley's version, and the valley had carried it thirty-two years and knew where every handhold was.

* * *

In July of 1865, seven men left this door at half past five in the morning. Whymper, the Englishman, who had tried the mountain eight times from the Italian side; Lord Francis Douglas, nineteen, a soft-spoken boy who had done a new route on the Obergabelhorn the week before and whom the guides liked, which was rarer than the guidebooks make out; the Reverend Charles Hudson, the strongest amateur of his age; young Hadow, nineteen also, Hudson's friend, with one season's experience, which mattered after; Michel Croz of Chamonix, a guide other guides watched to learn; and the two Peters, Taugwalder the father, forty-five, of this village, and Taugwalder the son, twenty-two, of this village, who on fine mornings still takes his coffee at the third table there by my dining-room window.

They found the mountain easier than thirty years of legend had promised, that is the terrible hinge of it, and at a quarter before two the next afternoon all seven stood on the summit, and the Italians, coming up the far side, were still hundreds of feet down, and the English party rolled stones to let them know the mountain was taken. One hour they had up there. It is a good summit, gentlemen; I have never stood on it; the men who have say you can see this hotel.

On the descent, Croz went first, then Hadow, then Hudson, then Lord Douglas, then the elder Taugwalder, then Whymper, then the son. On the difficult shoulder, above the north face, Hadow's boot went out. He took Croz over; the rope snapped Hudson from his hold, then Douglas. Four men were on the face. The elder Taugwalder braced against rock with the rope about his body, and the rope came taut, and held, and then did not hold. It parted between Taugwalder and Douglas, and the three who remained watched the four who did not, sliding, then falling free, four thousand feet to the glacier, and I will not describe that further, and no one who was there ever would.

Three days later the men of this village went out at dawn, my own father among them, and brought back what the glacier surface offered: Croz, Hudson, Hadow. The English chaplain read the service. Of the young lord there was found a boot, a belt, a pair of gloves. His mother wrote to this village every year until she was too old, asking whether the mountain had given anything more. The mountain has not.

* * *

Here Imboden paused and permitted the parlor its silence, and then, because he was a hotelier and not a dramatist, he opened the case and laid the rope along the table runner under the lamp, and let the fact do what facts do.

I had seen it through the glass a dozen evenings. I had not seen it *out*, and out was different. It was perhaps four feet of it, three strands, manila, gone the brown of old harness, and it was thin. That is the whole testimony. You have seen stouter cord on a butcher's parcel. Every rope in the guides' room across the street that season, 1897, would have held a fallen horse; this thing had been asked to hold four falling men, and the frayed end of it, teased out like a paintbrush left in a jar, said what it had said on the mountain.

"There were on that climb," said Imboden, re-coiling it with the handling of a sexton, "two hundred feet of good English rope, the Alpine Club's own pattern, and more of a second quality, also sound. And there was this, an old line, brought for spare, for fixing to rocks and leaving behind. When the party roped for the descent, this is what stood between the old guide and the young lord. At the inquiry the question was asked. Why the weak rope, there, of all places on earth?"

The cotton man, who had been good as gold for a quarter hour, could not help himself. "And the answer?"

"The answer of the inquiry," said Imboden, "was that there being insufficient good rope for all intervals when the party joined into one column, the old line was used for the last interval, without design, in the ordinary confusion of a great day. The elder Taugwalder was examined and acquitted of every imputation. That is the answer of the inquiry, and this house has never permitted any other under its roof." He shut the case. His voice did not change at all, which was how you knew the next sentence was the load-bearing one. "The answer of the London drawing rooms was otherwise. It was whispered, then printed with the whisper's courage, in the form of a question, which is how such things are always printed: whether an old peasant, seeing what was coming to a party of gentlemen, might not have chosen for his own interval a rope that would... release him from their company. No court said it. No signature carried it. It required none. Peter Taugwalder the elder was the second man ever to stand on that mountain, and he finished his life having it explained to him, by silence in this street, in the room of the guides, at the tables of the hotels, that he was either a fool or a murderer, with the choice left civilly open."

"He went to America," I said.

"For some years. Yes. There is a kind of talk a village cannot make and cannot stop, Herr Joiner, and an ocean is the one answer to it. It did not answer. He came home, and guided small, and died on the path above Findelen nine years ago, and the men who carried him down were the sons of the men who had gone silent, and every one of them would have given a season's wages to have the silence back. A village knows what it did, gentlemen, long after it has lost the power to undo it. You will find us very attentive, in these matters, ever since. It is not virtue. It is the scar."

He locked the cabinet and announced, in the same breath, that the barometer was rising and the dining room would open at seven, because that is how a hotelier tells a parlor the audience is ended.

* * *

The barometer kept its word. Two mornings later I finally made Number 78 of the list, *The Guides of Zermatt*, nineteen black coats in front of the guides' room wall, a picture that wanted an hour of diplomacy for every minute of optics, the question of who stood nearest whom in the front rank involving precedence structures older than some cantons. Taugwalder I put at center by the simple protocol of asking him first and letting the rest arrange themselves about the fact. He stood the exposure like church.

Afterward, while Clemenz cased the camera and the coats dispersed to their engagements, Peter Taugwalder the younger did not disperse. He waited until the street had taken the others, examining my tripod head as if pricing it, and then said, in the deliberate English of a man who has carried gentlefolk for forty years:

"You do not ask me things. All summer. The other photographers, always, they ask me things."

"You've been asked enough, I judged."

He considered that, turning the tripod's brass wing nut one way and the other. "You go much to the Zmutt," he said. "You and the lady of the stakes." It was not a question, so I did not answer it, and my silence was itself an answer, and we both knew the shape of what we were not saying, there in the emptying street. Half the village, I later understood, had drawn its own conclusions about the survey years since, and had been keeping the lady's secret for her with the terrible discretion of the scarred.

"I will tell you one thing," said Taugwalder. "Not for your firm. For your notebook, if you keep an honest one." He set the tripod gently against the wall, and stood straight, and delivered it as men deliver testimony they have not been believed on for thirty years, without heat, the heat having been used up somewhere in the 1860s.

"On the shoulder, when we roped down, I stood beside my father. I saw his hands. He tied the young lord with that rope *twice about the body*, twice, with the knot doubled, because the lord was light, and my father would have an extra turn on a light man. You do not give a double turn of any rope to a man you mean to lose." He picked up his rucksack. "The inquiry wrote it down and the drawing rooms did not print it. Rooms print the question, never the hands. My father's hands were the truest thing on that mountain, Herr Joiner. I saw them. I am the last alive who saw them."

He nodded once, the interview concluded by the same authority that had convened it, and went up the street to his Englishman, and I stood by the guides' wall a long moment with my own hands still smelling of brass and settled one thing finally in my mind, which I carried to the Riffelalp that same evening and put on Alexandra Forsyth's table beside her father's tables.

If the ice at the bottom held the boy, the ice held the rope's other end, and the rope's other end held the knots, and the knots were the last honest witnesses of the fourteenth of July, 1865: the question the drawing rooms had printed, answerable at last, in three turns of thirty-year-old manila.

"Then we are agreed on the order of things," she said, when she had heard me out. "My father's arithmetic brings us to the spot. Your optics tell us the hour. But whatever the ice renders, Mr. Joiner, the first reading of it belongs to neither of us." She looked out her north window, toward the village far below, where the lamps were coming on. "It belongs to the third table by the dining-room window."
