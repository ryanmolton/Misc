# 7. Boulders, Numbered

The Zmutt valley runs west out of Zermatt along the mountain's northern foot, and it was the part of the country I came to first love and last understand, which is a sequence I recommend. An hour's walk up from the village the path goes through the hamlet of Zmutt itself, a dozen larch houses black with three centuries of sun, standing on their staddle stones with their little barns among them, the whole settlement the size of a thought, and beyond it the valley opens and goes gray: moraine, milk-white river, and then the ice.

Nobody had told me about the sound. The Matterhorn glacier comes down off the north face in a broken apron, and the north face works on it all day, and four or five times in an afternoon there would come a crack like a quarry shot and then a long push of thunder while some cornice or serac went down the wall in a smoke of snow, and the first time it happened I had my head under the focusing cloth, and I record that Number 90 of the new series was very nearly a study of the photographer's own boot.

The shot list gave me four numbers in that valley. I took Clemenz and a tent, meaning to work it properly over three days, and it was on the first morning, on the crest of the old lateral moraine with the tripod half spread, that a voice behind me said, with an edge on it you could have shaved with:

"You are standing on my datum."

I looked down. Between my boots was a patch of white paint the size of a saucer, with a number on it: 22.

"I beg your pardon," I said, and moved, and the owner of the voice came past me without further examination, knelt at the paint, and set the heel of a surveyor's staff on it as though returning a borrowed tool to its peg. She was somewhere in her thirties, in gray tweed cut for work, with a canvas satchel gone white at the seams from rock, and she wore no hat against all local custom, her dark hair pinned up with the efficiency of a woman who has renegotiated her treaty with the wind and won. A hundred yards off, at a small theodolite on a tripod better than mine, a village boy stood waiting with the patience of the paid.

She took her sight, called a string of figures to the boy in French, wrote in a notebook bound in oilcloth, and only then looked at me again, at the camera, at Clemenz under his mountain of cases, and performed the whole examination in about a second and a half.

"You'd be the new view man," she said. Scots, the words squared off at the corners like ashlar. "The hotel said Chicago had sent someone." Her eyes went over my equipment the way a customs officer's do, looking for the lie in the manifest. "You people are early this year. The picture-postal trade doesn't usually infest this side before July."

"The infestation is four numbers and three days, ma'am, and I'll keep off your paint."

"Ma'am," she repeated, as though tasting a foreign coin. "Miss Forsyth will do. And it isn't the paint that concerns me, it's the practice. Your predecessors' idea of an improving view was to pose a client on stake fourteen with an ice axe he couldn't spell, kick the stake straight after, and cost me a season's figures. One of them," she said, with the level tone of a woman reporting an atrocity to a tribunal, "moved a boulder."

Clemenz, behind me, made a small noise of a man storing the story for the village.

"Moved it where?"

"Into his composition." She let that sit. "Nine hundredweight if it was a stone. He had four porters and no shame. The boulder was numbered, Mr. —"

"Joiner."

"— Mr. Joiner, it was numbered thirty-one, it had been numbered thirty-one for eleven years, and its whole use on this earth was that nobody moved it but the ice." She closed her notebook with a snap. "So you'll forgive the reception. What are your four numbers, and where do they stand?"

I told her: the mountain and glacier from the moraine crest, the hamlet, the ice grotto at the snout if the season had made one, and the Staffelalp prospect with the north face entire. She heard the stations the way a landlady hears references, and evidently they were respectable, because the edge came off, one full degree.

"The crest station's yours till noon, the light goes flat after. Keep to the crest line walking out; the inner slope is undercut this month and it goes without asking." She turned to leave, then paid me the compliment of a professional afterthought, thrown over the shoulder. "And if your glass is any good, take the ice at the snout while you're down there. It's doing something this summer it has not done in thirty years, and nobody in Europe is photographing it, which is the usual arrangement between events and photographers."

She was fifty yards gone before I understood I had been given a professional courtesy, however sharpened at both ends, and it wanted returning. I am my mother's son; I called after her.

"What did the boulder cost you?"

She stopped. Came back the fifty yards, which I later learned was the most Alexandra Forsyth ever conceded to anybody's curiosity, and looked at me properly for the first time, as a person rather than a trade.

"Eleven years of one line of figures," she said. "Which is eleven years of one man's evenings. My father plotted that stone through four hundred readings. The curve of its travel was the cleanest we had, and a fool in a boater bent it in an afternoon, and every paper we published after has an asterisk on it. That is what it cost. You're the first of your profession to ask." She considered me a moment longer. "The ice moves, Mr. Joiner. That surprises nobody; the peasants have known it for centuries, the physicists for sixty years. What my father gave his life to was the *rate*, the rate to the inch, season against season, year against year. To your trade this valley is scenery. It is in fact a machine, running slow, and we are measuring it."

"How fast does it run? Here, at the middle of the tongue."

"This month, about nine inches a day. High summer, a foot and more." She said it the way other women of her nation say the catechism. "Everything that stands on this ice, everything that has fallen into it, everything the mountain has fed it since before your republic and mine had names, is on its way down this valley at that rate. It is the slowest freight service in Europe, and it loses nothing. It only delivers late."

The boy at the theodolite called something then, weather coming over the Col d'Hérens, and she was Miss Forsyth of the survey again inside a second, notebook open, courtesy concluded.

"Miss Forsyth," I said, because there is a species of question that walks out of a man before his good sense can invoice it. "Did you know my predecessor? Fell, the man was called. He worked this valley eleven summers, and the last anyone heard of him, last August, he was photographing your glacier. The snout, from this moraine. Eleven plates of it."

I have spent my working life watching faces arrange themselves, it is half the craft of portraiture, and I watched hers do it now: watched something come up fast behind her eyes, look out at me, and be replaced by ordinary weather so smoothly that a man without my training would have sworn nothing had crossed at all. She squared her notebook against her satchel, in the manner of a person squaring more than paper.

"Everyone in the valley knew Mr. Fell by sight," she said. "Good day, Mr. Joiner. Mind the crest going out."

She went off up her line of numbered stones, calling figures, and the boy swung the little brass instrument after her like a compass needle finding its north again, and Clemenz and I stood alone on eleven thousand years of piled rubble with the mountain going smoky overhead.

Clemenz shouldered the cases. He had followed the entire exchange with the frank enjoyment of a village that has no theater and needs none.

"The Fräulein Doktor," he said, with reverence. "Two summers she is here. She pays cash, she falls never, and she is rude to everybody, in alphabetic order." He settled the straps, nodded up the moraine after her. "But you, Herr Joiner, she is rude to *specially*. This is a very good sign."

"Of what?"

"This I do not know," said Clemenz radiantly. "With ladies of quality I know only the signs, never the weather."

We made the crest number before the light flattened, and the hamlet in the afternoon, and that evening from the tent door I watched the survey work its way home down the far moraine, two small figures against a mile of gray, stopping at every patch of paint, and I thought about a curve bent in an afternoon, and about evenings, four hundred of them, one man's, and I confess the trade I thought hardest about that night was not my own. We had a vault of eleven hundred cabinets in Chicago and called it the visible world. This woman's father had spent eleven years on one stone, to learn one true number, and there was no vault anywhere that sold it.

The last thing I did by lantern light was open my notebook to the page where I had copied Fell's margin, the ruled line, the two dates thirty-one years apart, and under it I now wrote the day's acquisitions in a column, as the firm had trained me.

*Ice at the tongue: 9 in./day, rising.*
*It loses nothing. It only delivers late.*
*She knew him. She said she knew him by sight.*

Then, because the firm had trained me, I drew the line under the column where the sum goes, and left the sum blank, and blew out the light.
