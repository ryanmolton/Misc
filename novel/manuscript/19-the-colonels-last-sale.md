# 19. The Colonel's Last Sale

He met us below Zmutt, where the path comes down through the larches and a man standing in the shade of them commands the way as well as any customs house, and he had brought three men up with him: two porters engaged at the lower end of the village, and a narrow young man in a dust coat whom I identified at forty yards, by the tripod on his shoulder, as a photographer, and at twenty, by the newness of the tripod, as a Geneva man hired by the week. The Colonel himself stood forward in the path in the pale suit, hat in hand before the party the way undertakers stand, a posture I do not doubt he had rehearsed, and he let the mule and the litter come nearly to him before he spoke, and when he spoke the molasses was at full pour.

"Gentlemen. Ladies. A solemn morning, and I would not profane it. But there is such a thing as the public's sacred interest in a matter thirty years its own, and there is such a thing, sirs, as history's claim, and I stand here, humbly, in history's name—"

"Stand out of history's road," said Alexandra Forsyth, from beside the litter, in the voice that had cleared moraines, and I believe with hindsight that the whole affair might have been concluded by that sentence alone, for the Colonel's porters, who were valley men, had already seen what the litter was. They had their hats off before she finished, both of them, off and against their chests, and they backed out of the path and up onto the bank with their eyes down, the way men leave a church pew, and no wage on earth was going to put them back in the road, and a colonel without porters in that country is a man in a pale suit standing alone at seven thousand feet, a long way from Memphis.

He held his ground regardless, I will give the old buzzard that much for the record, and he shifted to the flank where his strength was. "Then I'll trouble nobody's feelings here," he said, sweeping the hat toward the valley, the wire office, the world. "The story tells itself by telegraph, with me or without me, and London pays by the question. You know my Saturday terms, Mr. Joiner. They have ripened."

"Before you wire London, Colonel," said Ambrose Fell, "ask your dispatch case what it's carrying."

I had wondered how the Colonel would meet Fell, the legend he had traded on, dealt with in *small dealings*, and plainly not expected ever to see standing on a mountain path behind a litter. What he did was go still, the whole performance of him, for two full seconds, which for such a man is a public confession, and then reassemble the warmth several sizes smaller.

"Ambrose. They said you were dead."

"They said you were a colonel. Open the case."

He did not open the case. His thumb went to the latch of it, one small involuntary tenderness, the way a man's tongue goes to a doubtful tooth, and that told everyone in the path everything about where the buckle rode.

"You bought it Tuesday," said Fell, in the dry level voice of a man reading an invoice aloud. "Eleven francs, beaten down from thirty, off a shepherd too poor to argue. Silver-gilt, monogrammed to the correct initials, gravel in the keeper, glacier polish on the face. I'll tell you what your Geneva man there can tell you tonight with a loupe if you stand him a lamp: the underside carries a Birmingham assay mark, and the date letter is 1871. Six years after the fall, Colonel. The finest expert in London will read it in under a minute, and so will the second finest, and they are both of them on the first train the moment you wire your question, because your forty correspondents don't travel alone. You've spent thirty years keeping this valley's grief at a simmer, out of the daylight, in velvet cases, where no man of learning ever looks twice. Send that wire, and daylight arrives by the trainload, and it examines everything, Colonel. The relic in your case. The certificates in your ledger. The four hundred feet you've retailed of a two-hundred-foot rope. You are the one man in Europe who cannot afford the crowd you're threatening us with, and you have been that man since Tuesday, and you paid eleven francs for the privilege."

The path was quiet. Somewhere down the valley the Sunday bells were still at it. Colonel Byron K. Prewitt stood in the larch shade doing sums, visibly, rapidly, an old machine and no rust anywhere in it, whatever else was wrong with the works, and I watched him arrive at the remainder, check it once, and accept the figure, all inside ten seconds, and I confess a purely technical admiration. I have seen honest men take a week to swallow less.

"The shepherd," he said at last, meditatively, to no one, in the dry Memphis voice. "Gravel in the keeper. He wept at fifteen francs, that boy. Wept." He looked past us all at Clemenz, at the head of the mule, whose face was a monument of alpine innocence, and something moved under the silver mustache that had every appearance of professional salute, one horse trader to the stable that had finally sold him one. "Beautiful," said the Colonel. "Beautiful."

He put his hat back on and squared it, and made his last offer with the trim of a man conceding a hand but not the table, because such men do not own another manner and it is idle to expect one at their age.

"There'd still be a fortune in it, done respectful," he said. "Books closed, twenty years hence. Memoirs. *The Glacier's Secret, by One Who Was Present.*"

"Colonel," I said, "you weren't present."

He inclined the hat a half inch, granting the point as a debating matter merely, and stood aside off the path, because whatever else the man knew, he knew a closed market at sight. And as the litter came past him, Byron Prewitt took the hat off again, all the way off, and held it to his chest until the mule and its burden and the whole silent company had gone down out of the larches, and I would set it down as performance and be done, except that his eyes at that moment were on none of us who could be performed at, and a lifetime in the reading of faces obliges me to report that for the length of that passing his own had gone empty of salesmanship entire, and old, and I do not know what was in it, and I have made it a rule in this account to say so when I do not know.

* * *

The rest of the Colonel is quickly told. He came down to the village that afternoon and was met at the door of his pension by its keeper, with his trunks already corded in the passage behind her, the village having concluded its own proceedings that morning by the means villages own; and at the Monte Rosa desk, where he presented himself with some notion of a farewell drink on the veranda, Herr Imboden regarded him across the register for a moment and then said, in English, pleasantly, with the finality of a barometer, "We have no season for you, sir. Nor has Lucerne," and that second clause was the true sentence of execution, for the hotel men of that country are a lodge, and word had gone down the valley wires before the Sunday bells stopped, and a memorial-goods man the Seiler list has closed its verandas to is a man whose trade is over from Chur to Chamonix, and both of them knew it, and no voice was raised at all.

He left on the Tuesday morning train. I was on the platform, seeing off the Basle freight, and he came along it in the pale suit with his cases on a barrow and stopped, because such men always stop, an exit unwitnessed being to them a form of death.

"Your Geneva boy," I said, "was owed a week he'll now not work. I've bought his plates and his time; the firm can use a second camera at the Gornergrat works. You'll want to settle his hotel, though."

"Settled at dawn," said the Colonel, wounded. "I have never in my life stiffed labor. Capital, yes; labor, never. A man must bank somewhere." He looked up the street, past the hotels, to where the mountain stood in its Tuesday morning weather, ordinary as bread, with two ravens rowing across the face of it, and for a moment I thought the emptiness might come back into his face, but the works had recovered themselves, and what came instead was the trade voice, softened one register, the nearest thing to a confession the machinery could produce. "Thirty years I retailed that mountain's sorrow, Mr. Joiner, and I'll tell you the queer part free of charge, as one operator to another: there was never a season the demand slackened. Never one. The stock of grief in this world," said Colonel Byron K. Prewitt, stepping up into the carriage and settling the planter's hat for the descent to Visp, "is the only inventory that replenishes itself, and the only one I ever handled that I couldn't water."

The train took him down the valley, and the valley, I am able to report across the distance of forty years, has never once missed him, and neither have I, and I have thought of him often, which is a different thing, and the distinction is the most he is owed.
