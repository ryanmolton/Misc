# 14. The Season

I came back over the Simplon road on the sixth of August, by diligence from Lake Maggiore, up through the gorges with an opera company bound for the Rhône valley and down the other side with most of their repertoire, and found Zermatt at full boil. August is the crown of the season. The street was a slow river of alpenstocks. The hotels had cots in the reading rooms. The Gornergrat railway, then building, had its cuttings full of Italian laborers and its future full of leader-writers arguing whether a rack railway to a viewpoint was the summit of civilization or its grave, a question the shareholders had already settled to their satisfaction. Six parties a day were going up the Matterhorn by the ropes and ladders of the Hörnli route, at which Edward Whymper's mountain had become, the English climbers said into their soup, a *via ferrata* for bankers, and the bankers came down sunburnt and alive and tipped like emperors, so the village bore the indignity with fortitude.

Three items of correspondence waited at the Monte Rosa, and I set them down in the order of ascending weather.

The first was from my mother. The school year was arranged; my father had taken a contract for the interior joinery of a new bank in Redlands and pronounced the architect *young but curable*; Rutherford, of the Coronado, to whom I had sent a card of the Rigi sunrise, had written her, they being by some freemasonry of the trade acquainted, to say her son photographed like a preacher who had seen the place himself. She required of me: flannel next the skin above five thousand feet, the name of the Scottish lady mentioned suspiciously often in my letters, and no heroics. The word *heroics* was underlined with the same stroke she used for failed grammar.

The second was from Stroud, a night-letter, and I transcribe it faithfully because nothing I could write would render him better:

TWENTY ONE NUMBERS OUTSTANDING SEASON SHORTENING STOP ITALIAN FREIGHT CHARGES NOTED ALSO CHARGES ZMUTT MULES JULY WHAT IS AT ZMUTT REQUIRING ELEVEN MULE DAYS ANSWER STOP NO WORD MERCHANDISE QUERY STOP SPEND LESS EXPLAIN MORE STROUD

The third had no stamp. It had been left at the desk by hand, in an envelope of a quality the village stationer did not stock, and it contained the Colonel's card, MEMPHIS · LUCERNE · THE WORLD, and across the back, in a fine commercial flourish:

*Welcome back from the sea, sir. We really must compare notes on the picturesque before the summer gets away from us both. Time and ice wait for no man. — B.K.P.*

I stood at the desk reading that twice, and the second reading did what the first had declined to do. *Time and ice.* It might have been the small talk of the season; the whole village discussed glaciers at dinner the way coastal towns discuss tides. It might have been. I went down to my cellar with a lamp before I had taken off my coat.

The lock was where I had left it, and it opened as it had always opened, and that told me what I needed, because I had left it, deliberately, since June, with the shackle turned a half-turn short of home, a joiner's son's habit, my father having taught me that a lock tells you more about your visitors than it refuses them. It was turned fully home now. Some tidy soul had locked up conscientiously on his way out.

Inside, nothing was missing. My stock of plates stood in their cases; the firm's negatives, catalogued and crated for Basle, stood sealed; the eleven prints from Fell's plates hung on the line where any reasonable man would have left drying prints, because a thing on a line looks like process, and hiding it would have made it evidence, and I had reasoned in June that the cellar's best safe was its own boring plainness. I had reasoned like a man with a lock, and not like a man with a visitor who could read. The prints hung in their eleven gray near-identical ranks, and I stood in front of them with the lamp up, trying to see them as a stranger in a hurry would see them, and the exercise steadied me until my eye reached the seventh print, and stopped, and the steadiness went out of the cellar.

They were re-pegged in the wrong order. Not shuffled; *re-ordered*. Whoever had taken them down had put them back with the care of a man restoring appearances, and he had restored them left to right by the depth of the shadow in the ice cave, darkest to lightest, which is how a picture man sorts prints when the dates scratched on the negatives mean nothing to him and he is working fast, by lamplight, from visual weight. It was a good, professional, fluent restoration, and it was wrong, and it had been wrong for perhaps three weeks while I was in Italy, and the man who had made it had therefore stood where I was standing, long enough to take down eleven prints, study them, and reach whatever an old fraud reaches in place of the truth.

He could not fuse the pairs; there was no viewer in the cellar, and no reason on earth for him to imagine one wanted. He could not read the dates. What he could read, better than any man in the Valais, was the commercial signature of the whole arrangement: a first-class operator exposing eleven plates of a worthless subject and a famous man vanishing rather than explain himself. Prewitt had spent thirty years retailing the mourning of this valley. He knew the story of the unrecovered lord the way a broker knows a bond he has clipped coupons off half his life, and he knew, from the Bossons tale, which every relic man in the Alps kept in stock as patter, that glaciers pay out. He did not have the science, the dates, the window, or the spot. He had eleven gray prints, a welcome-back card, and a nose that had never in thirty years pointed wrong at money, and I judged, standing in my violated cellar, that it was pointing now, and that we no longer owned the only clock in the valley.

* * *

Alexandra Forsyth heard my report of the Italian journey on the Riffelalp terrace at seven the next morning, at a corner table she had chosen against eavesdroppers with a field commander's eye, and she heard the letter from Fell read aloud twice, and pronounced her judgment on the man in one sentence, which was her ration.

"He waited thirty-one years, and then salted the road behind him so the right stranger could catch him up: that is either the loneliest man in Europe or the sanest, and I no longer see the daylight between." Then she spread her August sheets on the marble and gave me the glacier's news, which was the morning's real business, and it was this: the ice had come on.

The summer of 1897 was running hot. Her stake lines, resurveyed the Monday past, showed the tongue moving at rates her father's tables held for the steepest weeks of record seasons, fourteen inches a day at mid-line, and the melt to match. The snout was wasting backward even as the mass bore forward, the cave mouth had doubled its arch since July, and the bright grain in Fell's prints, which we had plotted together in her sitting room before Italy, riding its buried line, was coming due. She had run the sum four ways against four rate assumptions. The envelopes all closed in the same fortnight.

"The twentieth of August," she said, "give or take five days either side, most of the weight to the far side of it. If this weather holds. If the föhn comes," and she looked south, over her spectacles, at a sky as clean as new glass, "sooner. A warm wind at this stage is worth a week in a night." She began folding her sheets in the order she folded them every time, a woman to whom sequence was a form of prayer. "My letter went to Scotland the Friday you left. Registered, to the family's man of business in Edinburgh, marked private, saying what the science says and asking the family's instruction. No answer can reach here before the twelfth at the earliest. We are now in the fortnight, Mr. Joiner, where everything we do not control begins arriving in whatever order it pleases."

"One thing arrived already," I said, and told her about the cellar.

She listened without interrupting, which from her was a form of alarm, and at the end she was quiet a moment, looking down the valley to where the village lay in its morning smoke.

"Thirty years of mourning with a railway to it," she quoted at last. "You will go up the west path tonight?"

"Tonight."

"Then tell Mr. Fell his season of working alone is over whether he consents or not; the competition has seen his plates. And Mr. Joiner." She fixed me over the spectacles with the look that had cleared the moraine of view-peddlers for two summers. "You'll be past the Staffel pastures by dark. Take the boy as far as the alp and no farther, keep off the skyline where the path crosses the crest, and carry no lantern going up. Everyone in this valley watches lights on that side after nightfall. It is how the village knows its poachers, and we are the poachers now."

* * *

Clemenz walked me out that evening as far as the last shoulder before the alp, carrying nothing, for once, but his own supper, aggrieved at half-shares in the adventure and consoling himself aloud, in the twilight, with the intelligence he had gathered and now formally rendered: that his cousin the shepherd sold, every second day, to a solitary English gentleman lodging in the old cheese hut above Staffel, two litres of milk, bread when there was bread, and once, memorably, a rope's length of dried sausage, paid always in silver, always with thanks, and always, said the cousin, with the same joke, that he was watching the ice for the government, which the cousin did not believe, the government paying nobody in silver.

At the shoulder Clemenz stopped, as ordered, and looked past me up the darkening path, to where it climbed toward the black shapes of the alp against the last green band of the sky, and for once in our acquaintance the hotel vocabulary failed to answer and he stood turning his cap in his hands.

"You will say to him," he managed at last, "that the village never took the room apart. Herr Imboden keeps it. The chair waits. You will say it like that, the chair waits, he will understand it."

"I'll say it."

He nodded and went down, and I turned up the path alone, no lantern, the dew coming into the grass, the great wall of the mountain to my left going from rose to ash the way iron cools, and after the first mile there was no sound in that whole high world except the river far under everything, and my own boots, and once, from the ice, a single dry crack traveling the dark like news.
