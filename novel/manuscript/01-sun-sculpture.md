# 1. Sun Sculpture

The Hotel del Coronado photographed best at seven in the morning, before the sea wind came up and before the guests came down to stand in front of things. I had learned this over four days of trying. The hotel was a great white heap of shingle and turret, roofed in red, built all of wood by men who must have believed fire was something that happened to other people, and at seven the light came across the bay low and warm and picked out every rail and bracket of it, so that the whole absurd wedding cake of a building looked carved rather than carpentered.

I was twenty-six that spring, and I had spent three years of my life making pictures of things for people who would never go and see them. That was the business. Stroud Brothers of Chicago published stereoscopic views, boxed sets of them, one hundred cards to a set, and sold them door to door across the whole of the United States by an army of college boys on commission. A card is a simple thing to describe. Two photographs of the same subject, made side by side through two lenses set as far apart as a pair of human eyes, mounted together on a curve of gray board. Looked at flat, they are twins, and dull ones. Looked at through the stereoscope, the two views pour together and stand up off the card, and the parlor wall falls away, and a person in Dubuque who will never in this life hold a steamship ticket is standing on the rim of the Grand Canyon with the morning cold coming up out of it.

The catalogue called our views Sun Sculptures. That was Mr. Stroud's phrase, and he was proud of it, and for once the salesmanship was honest. A flat photograph is a picture of a thing. A stereograph is the thing, or near enough that your body believes it a little, which is a different article entirely and worth a dollar a dozen of anybody's money.

That morning I was making negative number nine of a set to be called *California: The Golden Shore*, and the hotel's assistant manager for publicity, a Mr. Loper, was standing at my elbow ruining it.

"You'll want the flag in," said Mr. Loper.

"The flag is in."

"Sharp, I mean. Snapping. Can you have it snapping?"

"I can have it snapping or I can have it sharp," I said. "The wind moves it and the plate takes its time. Which would you rather?"

He thought about this seriously, as though I had offered him a choice of entrées. Mr. Loper was a young man with a mustache he had grown to seem older and a straw hat he wore to seem gayer, and both articles worked against each other. "Sharp," he said at last. "A blurred flag looks like weather coming."

"There's no weather coming."

"Looks like it, though. In the picture. People study these things through the glass, Mr. Joiner, they see everything." He was right about that, at least. Through the lens of a stereoscope a customer sees more of a place than most visitors ever do standing in it. I have had letters forwarded from the firm, care of the caption department, asking after the name of a dog asleep on a porch in negative number forty-one, set eleven, *Old Mexico*.

Mr. Loper's other request that week had been the tent city. South of the hotel, along the strand, there stood several acres of white canvas where families who could not afford the Del's tariff boarded by the week, bathed in the same ocean, and by every report had considerably more fun. The hotel wished the tents kept out of the views. I had kept them out. It was not my business what a hotel wished to admit about itself, and anyway the tents would get their own card someday, in some cheaper set, and be loved by exactly the people who had stayed in them.

I made the exposure at ten past seven. The flag, for the record of anyone studying the matter through a lens, is sharp.

* * *

I should say what I looked like standing there, since it bears on certain furniture of this story, though less than you might suppose and less than it bore on where I slept. I am tall, and I am colored, as the word went then, my father being a black man of Tennessee and my mother a white woman of Ohio, and what the mixture had produced was a man brown enough that hotels solved the problem of me in a variety of ways, each presented as a courtesy. The Del's particular courtesy was a clean, narrow room in the staff annex, back of the laundry, while the firm was billed for accommodations at the guest rate. I noted the difference in my expense book, in the column I kept for such arithmetic, which I had headed years before, in a mood I no longer entirely remembered, ITEM: AMERICA.

The annex had one advantage, and that was breakfast. The waiters ate before service, at a long table by the kitchen, and they ate what the chef was proudest of and not what moved slowest from the larder, and after four mornings among them I had been adopted outright. A waiter named Rutherford, who had come out from New Orleans by way of a Pullman car, took a proprietary interest in my equipment and my prospects. He was sixty and had seen everything twice.

"What they pay you to make a picture of this hotel?" he asked me, the last morning.

"They pay me by the month. The hotel doesn't pay me anything. The hotel is a subject."

"A subject." He liked that. He looked out the annex door at the great red roof of the thing, turning the word over. "And these pictures sell in Chicago."

"Chicago, Cincinnati, Boston. Anywhere there's a parlor and a door to knock on."

"People in Boston buying pictures of a hotel they never stayed at, never will."

"That's the whole trade," I said. "You put the world in a box. The box goes on the parlor table. Sunday afternoon, the family passes the viewer around, and everybody travels. It's cheaper than the railroad and nobody gets seasick."

Rutherford considered this and poured me more coffee. "White folks," he said, "will buy anything flat and call it someplace." Which remains, of all the descriptions of the photographic industry I have heard in fifty years, the one requiring the fewest corrections.

* * *

The telegram found me at four o'clock that afternoon on the north veranda, cleaning plate holders. A boy in hotel livery carried it out on an actual silver tray, which tells you what the Del thought of telegrams, and stood by while I read it, in case there should be a reply, or a tip, or a scene.

It was from Chicago, from the office of Mr. E. O. Stroud himself, and it ran this way:

FELL SILENT SINCE AUGUST STOP ALPS SERIES INCOMPLETE STOP FINISH GOLDEN SHORE AND COME CHICAGO FIRST TRAIN STOP SAY NOTHING STOP E O STROUD

I read it twice and gave the boy a dime to disappoint him honestly.

Everyone in the trade knew Fell, and no one in America knew him. That is a riddle with a plain answer. The finest views the firm had ever issued, the sets that had built the Stroud name and half the Stroud building, went out into the world under the imprint alone. *The High Alps. The Holy Land. Egypt and the Nile.* The catalogue did not say who had stood behind the camera, because the catalogue's theory was that the firm was the artist, as a mill might claim to be the weaver. The man who had stood behind it, for most of thirty years and on four continents, was called Ambrose Fell. Operators spoke of him as apprentice masons speak of the man who set the keystone in the bridge they cross to work. I had been raised, in a manner of speaking, inside his pictures. There had been a stereoscope in my mother's parlor in Highland, California, and a set of the original *High Alps* got secondhand from a Redlands estate sale, and before I ever saw a mountain with snow on it I had stood on a dozen of them in a walnut viewer with my supper going cold. Number 87 of that set was the Matterhorn from the east, with a black-hatted guide standing on the moraine for scale, and I can tell you now, as a professional matter, that the guide stands exactly where he must stand for the ice to read as ice and the mountain to leap back four miles behind him. At nine years old I did not know why the card worked. I only knew that when I looked into it something in my chest went quiet.

Silent since August. It was March. A man does not go quiet for seven months on the strength of bad mails.

I sat with the telegram a while, and the afternoon went on around me in its expensive way. Croquet on the south lawn. A naval officer explaining the fleet to two women who were watching a third woman instead. The Pacific laying itself down on the strand over and over with that sound that is not quite applause. I thought about what SAY NOTHING meant coming from a man who owned a caption department, and I thought about seven months, and I confess that under the worry, where I could pretend not to see it, there was a bright vein of something that was not worry at all. The Alps series. Incomplete.

Mr. Loper came by at five to ask whether I might, before departing, make a view of the new electric launch, the hotel having lately electrified everything that would hold still for it.

"I'm called back to Chicago," I told him. "Urgent business of the firm."

"Nothing wrong, I hope."

"A man forgot to write home," I said. "It happens in the best families."

The light was still good, so I finished the plate holders, and made the launch for him anyway at six, with the water going pewter and the hotel behind it holding the last of the sun the way white wood does, gently, as if it had been asked to hold it for a friend who was coming right back. It was a good negative. It went out that autumn as number twelve of the set, and if you have the card, and a glass, you may observe at the launch's stern a man in a straw hat, blurred, waving at the camera against express instructions.

I packed my cases that night and caught the morning ferry, and the Del stood on the water behind me with its flags snapping, unphotographably sharp.
