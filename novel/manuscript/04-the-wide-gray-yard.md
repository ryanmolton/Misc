# 4. The Wide Gray Yard

The *Fürst Bismarck* took eight days to Southampton, and I passed them in the manner of second cabin, which is to say among people who could afford to cross the ocean but had been made to feel they should apologize for the way they did it, and were therefore friendlier than the people upstairs.

On the first afternoon out a gentleman from Rochester in a plaid ulster handed me his coat and hat at the door of the smoking room. I took them, considered the coat, judged the cloth, and handed them back.

"Decent wool," I said, "but the shoulders are cut too generous for you. Whoever sold it saw you coming."

There is a particular silence a man falls into while he re-sorts his entire understanding of a situation, and I had long since learned to wait through it comfortably, having caused a great deal of it in my time. The gentleman from Rochester came out the far side of the silence red as flannel, apologized more than was needed, and spent the remaining week overcorrecting, so that by the Fastnet light I knew the names of his three daughters and his opinion of free silver, and had twice declined the loan of the ulster against the deck wind. People are mostly not wicked. They are lazy, and they have bought their ideas the way he bought the coat, ready made, without asking about the shoulders.

The Germans of the crew, for their part, had settled the question of me at the gangway with one word, *Amerikaner*, filed under it whatever else they noticed, and moved on to matters of importance, such as whether the wind would let the tables be set with the good glasses. I found I liked being a nation instead of a problem. It was restful, in the way of any vacation, and like any vacation it would end at a border.

Mornings I walked the deck. Afternoons I worked. I had the shot list to learn, one hundred and twenty numbers with Fell's original station notes copied out beside sixty-one check marks, and I had a book, bought at a shop on lower Broadway the morning we sailed, called *Scrambles Amongst the Alps*, by Mr. Edward Whymper.

I record here only what a man could learn on a steamer's deck chair, because that was all I then knew, and a story ought to be honest about the order in which it learned things. Mr. Whymper was an engraver of London who in the 1860s had made himself the most determined mountaineer alive, and the great prize of that decade was the Matterhorn, which had turned back every party that tried it and had gathered about itself the reputation of a thing not meant to be had. In July of 1865 Whymper reached the top with six companions. It is the most famous mountain ascent ever made, and the reason it is famous is what happened on the way down. A rope broke. Four men went down the north face, a fall you may measure in thousands of feet: a Chamonix guide named Michel Croz, a clergyman named Hudson, a young man named Hadow, and a lord of nineteen named Francis Douglas. Whymper and two Zermatt guides, a father and son both named Peter Taugwalder, survived on the upper end of the broken rope.

The bodies were recovered from the glacier below some days after. Three of them were.

I read that chapter twice, the second time in that state of settled dread with which one reads a thing that has already happened and cannot be prevented by any amount of attention, and then I sat with the book shut on my knee and the wide gray yard of the Atlantic going by, and I thought about Number 87 in my mother's parlor, the mountain leaping back four miles behind the black-hatted guide, and how I had stood inside that card a hundred times at nine years old and had known nothing at all about what the ice at the bottom of it was holding.

The fourth man was never found. Whymper gives it one sentence, and shuts the sentence like a door.

The firm's London office was in High Holborn, a shopfront with a viewing parlor where the public might sample the merchandise in mahogany stereoscopes chained to the counter like city bibles, and a floor above it where Mr. Considine conducted the correspondence of three continents from within a weather system of paper entirely his own.

"Joiner, is it, Joiner, yes," he said, shaking my hand with both of his, moving a chair's cargo of catalogues to the floor for me. "The Coronado man. Lovely rail work in that number nine, lovely, the girls in mounting fought over it." He was fifty or so, Irish, collarless by four in the afternoon, and he talked as rain falls in his country, steadily, warmly, with no evident plan of stopping. I had been instructed I might keep whatever was useful. I kept nearly all of it.

The plates were cased and waiting, forty dozen, the new emulsion, and a letter of credit on a bank in Lausanne, and my rail bookings, Charing Cross to Paris to Lucerne. Business done, Considine took me down to the viewing parlor, locked the till out of ceremony rather than distrust, and stood me in front of the window display, where between a pyramid of *Egypt and the Nile* and a fan of *The Holy Land* sat the old *High Alps* box, its gilt gone tender at the corners.

"Now," he said. "You'll want Fell."

"Mr. Stroud said you'd talk."

"Mr. Stroud pays me partly for what I know and mostly so he needn't hear it himself." He took the Matterhorn card out of the box, Number 87, and dropped it in the rack of the nearest stereoscope, and motioned me to the eyepiece while he talked, so that the whole account came to me with the mountain standing in front of it, which I have always suspected he did on purpose. Considine was a salesman before he was a correspondent, and he knew a lesson wants a picture.

The account, shorn of its weather, ran so. Ambrose Fell had come to the firm in 1870, out of a failed portrait studio in Manchester, already the best technical man anyone had seen. In 1878 the brothers sent him to Switzerland for the set that would make everybody's fortune but his. He was nine months in the high country with a mule, a tent of rubbered canvas, and a boy from Chamonix to carry glass. The contract he had signed before sailing gave the firm, in language, said Considine, that would strip paint at forty years' distance, every negative, every right, every mention. Fell had signed it because he was thirty-eight years old and broke, which is the age and condition at which the trade gets everyone's signature.

"He never forgave the contract," I said.

"He never forgave *himself*," said Considine. "The contract he understood. Firms are firms the way weather is weather; you don't forgive a wet April, you buy a coat. No. What ate him was that he agreed. He sold his name cheerful as a man selling a horse he thinks is spavined, and then had to watch the horse win the Derby for twenty years running." He rapped the stereoscope with a knuckle. "That card there has been in a million parlors. A million, Joiner, I've seen the ledgers. Name one soul outside this trade who's heard of Ambrose Fell."

Through the eyepiece the mountain stood in its four miles of distance, and on the moraine the black-hatted guide waited where he had waited since 1878, the size of a beetle in creation, and I thought, not for the first time since the vault, that I did not know who the guide was either.

In 1889 Fell had gone as far as the door. A rival house, one of the new combinations, offered him his own imprint, his name in gold on the box lid. He had let them draw the contract, and then in the end had not signed, and no one knew why, though Considine had his theory, delivered at a lower pressure than the rest of him, almost gently.

"Marianne wouldn't have it. His wife. She had a horror of grand gestures, that woman, she'd seen what they cost. She told him a name on a box is still a box, and a quarrel dressed as a business is still a quarrel, and they'd only ever been happy when he was working and she was mending his socks in eleven countries. So he stayed. And they went on. It wasn't a sad life, mind. I'll not have you thinking it was a sad life. There was no man better company in Europe on his day."

"And then."

"And then she died. Winter of '94, in lodgings in Nice, of her heart, with no warning at all." Considine took the card out of the rack and squared it back into the box with the others. "He worked straight through it, which frightened me worse than drink would have. And when the brothers proposed the great re-making of the Alps set, the greatest compliment the house ever paid a living man, mind, though naturally they'd have printed the water company's name on it sooner than his, Ambrose wrote back one line. I saw the letter. 'Yes. I began there.'" He shut the box lid. "*I began there.* A man of fifty-six says that about a place, you're not sending an operator, Joiner. You're posting a letter to 1878."

The shop bell rang. Two ladies came in out of the Holborn drizzle to ask for views of the Italian lakes, and Considine went to them all warmth and Como, and I stood a while by the window among the boxed continents.

My boat train left in the morning. He came to the station anyway, talking, carried my case to the platform, and handed it up to me with the one piece of it all he had rehearsed, I think, as against the rest that merely rained.

"If you find him," said Considine, "don't stand between him and the work. Whatever it is. Stand behind the work and look useful. It's the only door into that man, and Joiner, listen to me, it's the only door out."
