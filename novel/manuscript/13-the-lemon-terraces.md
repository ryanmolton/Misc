# 13. The Lemon Terraces

The railway serves the Cinque Terre grudgingly, in the manner of a thing built through a country rather than to it, diving from tunnel to tunnel with the five villages doled out between like cards off a deck, a second apiece: a fistful of colored houses jammed in a crease of cliff, a strip of bright water, then rock again and the dark. I got down at the third card, at seven in the morning, into a heat with weight to it, and walked out of the little station and down a street the width of my outstretched arms, and the street opened, and there was Vernazza.

I had been paid for eleven years to look at places with a view to selling them, and the habit runs deep and I do not apologize for it: my first thought, entire and professional, was *the firm has nothing like this and would ruin it.* The town is one street and a hundred stairways. It pours down its ravine to a small stone-armed harbor, the one true harbor on that whole iron coast, and stops at the water as though caught mid-stride. The houses stand shoulder to shoulder at the quay, five and six stories, too tall for their width like books packed on end, and they are painted the colors bakers use, crust and crumb and burnt sugar and that rose the Italians get by letting red age forty years in salt air. On the seaward rock a round watchtower. On the harbor's other horn, a church with an octagonal belfry, gray and plain, its foundations in the waves' business. Nets on the quay. Lemons on the terraces above, going up the cliff in stone-walled steps to where the vines take over, a thousand years of wall, the whole mountainside worked by hand into a ladder of gardens, and over all of it a light that did not behave like my light. Alpine light comes down like judgment. This came off the water as well as the sky, from below as well as above, so that shadows filled with reflected sea and nothing on that waterfront was ever wholly dark. I stood at the street's mouth with my one case, working out the optics of it, and a woman above me cranked open a shutter and hung out a mattress to air, and looked down at me with frank interest and no alarm at all, and called something inquiring, of which I understood one word, because the word was *Ghiglione*.

The village had sorted my business before I had. I was a foreigner with a photographer's case, and the village had a category waiting, stitched and worn the winter long: another one belonging to the signore.

* * *

The Pensione Ghiglione was four rooms above a boat store, kept by the widow Ghiglione, a small square woman of sixty with forearms a smith would respect and a black dress gone soft gray at the seams, and she received me on her stairs, heard the name Fell in my first sentence, and did the thing I had crossed the Alps and half a kingdom being braced for. She looked at me a long moment, and nodded as though a delivery long expected had finally rounded the point, and said, in the French of the coast, which is Italian wearing a borrowed hat: "You will want coffee. He said you would come in the summer. He said you would be tired, and behind time, and honest, and I was to feed you before any talking." She turned up the stairs, then paused and inspected me again over her shoulder, item by item, and delivered the signore's full prophecy with a widow's scruple for the record. "He did not say you would be so tall."

That is how I learned that Ambrose Fell had left not a trail but a sieve, and that I was the grain that had come through it. The postal forwarding, standing unrevoked in a Swiss ledger where only a man tracing plates would ever ask; the box in the wardrobe with its lie lettered on the lid, a lie exactly transparent to one kind of reader; the room's effects in their eloquent order. He had baited the road at every fork with things only an operator would stop for, and the operator had stopped, and had printed the rejects at one in the morning as intended, and had crossed the Théodule as intended, and now stood being fed bread and anchovies and hill wine at eight in the morning as intended, by a widow with instructions eleven months old.

She fed me. Then she talked, three winters' worth.

He had come in November, half dead of a cough he called a nuisance, with a trunk and a case of books, and had taken the north room, the cheap one, sea glimpse from one corner of the window if you stood like a heron, and the village had him classified within the week: *il fotografo inglese*, elderly, courteous, mad, out on the breakwater before dawn in December watching the light happen with his hands in his pockets and no camera at all, which confirmed the madness and, in a fishing town, commanded a certain respect. The rehabilitation from mad to beloved had gone by the usual staircase, which is competence. He had mended her pump. He had rehung the shutter whose hinge-scream the whole quay knew by name. He had gone out with the boats twice, been sick once, learned the dialect words for the six kinds of wind, and paid his bill on Fridays in silver with the rent of a man who had noticed the room was cheap and quietly doubled it.

And in February, with the north room's light at its cleanest, he had unpacked the case that was not books after all, and begun, at close to sixty years of age, in a room eleven feet square smelling of tar and lemons, what Signora Ghiglione called *his true winter's work*, and what I, who was shown it that afternoon hanging in the houses of a fishing village, will go to my grave calling the finest sustained photography of the age, and no one in my trade has ever heard of it, and the trade's ignorance is the exact measure of everything the trade is.

He had photographed the village. That says nothing. Every excursionist with a hand camera photographs the village. He had photographed the village the way the village would have photographed itself if walls had eyes and mothers ran the printing frame: the widow's daughter at her loom with the shuttle blurred to a moth because he chose the loom's own exposure over a posed stillness; the men mending the great seine on the quay, sixty feet of net between eight pairs of hands, an entire economy legible in one look; old Berto the caulker asleep against his own upturned boat at noon, laid out with the dignity of a crusader on a tomb; and in house after house, up and down the stairways of that town, in tin frames on chests of drawers, the portraits. He had made portraits all winter, of anyone who fed him, and given the prints as rent for the feeding, and they hung now where the saints hang, and they deserved the placement. The signora's own was in the kitchen: her husband, drowned off Palmaria in 1881, existing until last winter as one waxen daguerreotype made at his conscription. Fell had copied it, and then, by darkroom means I partly reconstructed and partly still marvel at, had taken the tin stiffness out of the boy's face and the death out of the shadows and printed him warm, on matte paper the color of church candles, so that the man looked out of the frame at his widow across a kitchen he had never stood in and seemed on the point of asking what was for supper.

"He said," reported Signora Ghiglione, laying supper, "that it was the only work he ever signed."

It was true. In the corner of every print in that village, small as a grain of rice, in pencil: *A.F.*

* * *

The trunk was in the boat store below, under an old sail, corded and sealed, and she showed it to me as one shows a grave, and told me its standing orders: to be held until All Saints; if he had not returned or written by then, to go, unopened, to the priest, with money for Masses. What was in it I never learned, then or after, and I have thought about it on and off for the rest of my life, the way you think about a book seen once in a foreign shop window after hours, and I set that down here plainly so the reader is not waiting for the lid to open. It does not open. It was his, and it stayed his, and there is a whole popular theory of stories that calls this a loose end, and there is an older theory, my father's, the joiner's, that calls it a lock fitted to the one door in the house that is none of the visitors' business.

What was mine was a letter. She brought it down from behind the kitchen crucifix, where it had wintered, addressed in the narrow upright hand: *To the man the firm sends.*

Six lines.

*If you have come this far by the road I left, you have printed the eleven and understood them, and you are an operator, not a clerk. I am sorry for the clerk; the road was made to lose him. The work you are finishing is yours; I finished mine. What remains on the ice is not work and is not the firm's. If you want a hand in it, be at the place the plates were made after the first of August, and come up the west path, alone, in the evening, without the firm in your pockets. If you do not come I will think none the worse. Most debts are best left to the ice. — A.F.*

I read it on the breakwater at sunset, with the swifts going over the tower in their thousands and the sea doing its slow bright arithmetic against the stones, as far from ice as the continent could take me, and then I went up and asked the signora what I owed for the week, because the first of August was two days gone already, and the answer to Fell's letter had been decided somewhere north of Genoa days before, riding a night train with its forehead against the glass.

She took my money, most of it, for the room and what she called disturbances. Then at the door in the blue morning she put into my case, wrapped in a page of *Il Secolo XIX*, flat and stiff, a parting gift: one of the signore's prints, hers to give, she said, since he had made it in her own upstairs hallway and left it as too spoiled to sign.

I unwrapped it on the train. It is before me on the desk as I write this sentence, forty years on. It shows a door standing half open at the end of a whitewashed corridor, morning, the light coming through the gap in one blade across the tiles, and in the lit slice of the room beyond, on the floor, the shadow of a woman, her back to the door, arrested in some plain act, folding perhaps. Nothing else. There is no woman in the pensione it could have been; I asked, that morning, and the signora only looked at the print a while and then at me, and shrugged with her whole body, a shrug the size of the coast, and said what I have since decided is the last word available on the subject.

"He was a long time up there over the winter, signore. Doors are cheap. Light he had plenty. Whoever it is," and she wrapped it back up and tied the string, "she was folding, and content. Take it north where he can't be embarrassed by it."
