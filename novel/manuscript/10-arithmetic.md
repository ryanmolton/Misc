# 10. Arithmetic

Fell's station was where the prints said it would be, on the moraine crest two hundred paces down-valley from paint number 22, and Clemenz found the stones inside a quarter hour, three flats set level under two inches of gravel, with a chisel mark on the center stone for the tripod's leading foot. He knelt over them a long time, reading them the way his people read snow.

"Built to stay the winter," he said. "He means to come back, Herr Joiner. A man does not lay foundation for one season." He stood and dusted his knees and looked down the gray mile of rubble to the snout, where the river ran out of its blue arch, and I watched him do the sum I had been doing since three in the morning. Clemenz had carried for surveyors before he carried for me. He knew what a fixed station was for. "Or," he said slowly, "he means for the *picture* to come to *him*."

"What do you know about pictures coming to people?"

"I know the Fräulein Doktor asks every week the same question of the same stones," said Clemenz, "and the ice answers a little different every time, and one summer, my grandfather says, the answer arrives at the bottom." He shouldered the tripod. "You will want to talk to her."

"I will want to talk to her."

"She is at stake nine since seven o'clock," said Clemenz, in the tone of a concierge who has already sent up the card.

* * *

She heard me out on the open moraine with the wind pushing at both of us, and I gave it to her straight and in order, the box, the sleeve, the eleven dates, the margin ruled in the Whymper, because a woman who numbers boulders has earned a report in her own language, and when I finished she said nothing for the length of three gusts. Then she held out her hand for the folding viewer.

I had racked the pair on the walk up, July 19th against August 9th. She stood with her boots set apart, steadied the instrument against the wind as though it were her own theodolite, and looked, and I watched Alexandra Forsyth meet the thing I had met in the cellar. Her shoulders did not move. Her breathing did. When she lowered the viewer at last her eyes went straight down the moraine to the snout, to the arch, refocusing from the little walnut theater to the actual gray world that theater had just informed on, and her face had the carefulness of a person carrying a full vessel over uneven ground.

"How does it do that," she said. Not a question, an audit. I explained the principle in four sentences, the two views set apart in time instead of space, the instrument reading change as depth, whatever rides the ice standing forward off the dead rock. She nodded once at the theory, an examiner's nod at a proof she would re-derive herself that evening, and handed back the viewer, and said, "My father would have given ten years of his life for that instrument, and he had no ten to give. Come to the Riffelalp at four. Ask for my sitting room. We are past discussing this on open ground."

She was forty paces gone up her line before she turned and added, with the wind taking half of it, "And Mr. Joiner. Thank you for the report. It was properly made," which I record because from that quarter, that season, it was the Legion of Honor.

* * *

The Riffelalp hotel stood on its shelf above the valley, and her sitting room was the cheapest it had, north-facing, one window, no view a tourist would pay for, and every flat surface in it under paper: rolls of profile drawings, tables in a dead man's hand annotated in a living one, and on the wall, where another woman of her class would have hung a watercolor, a surveyed longitudinal section of the Matterhorn glacier, inked like an anatomy.

She poured tea without asking and put the milk in first, decisively, Scots fashion, and began in the middle, having evidently decided during the intervening hours where the middle was.

"My father read the letter on the fourteenth of July, 1866. One year to the day, which the newspapers enjoyed. You know the substance of the accident; the world does. What the world has forgotten is what could not be found. Three men were recovered from the glacier within the week and lie in Zermatt. The fourth, Lord Francis Douglas, nineteen years of age, was not recovered. A boot, a belt, a pair of gloves. Nothing else, then or since. The Alpine men said the rocks of the north face kept him. My father read the accounts, looked at his own work, and wrote to the Journal that the rocks keep nothing on that face; that the face feeds the glacier; and that the glacier is a conveyance with a schedule; and that ice moving at the observed rates would carry a burden from the foot of that wall to the snout in something between twenty-eight and thirty-six years, and would then, in his phrase, *render its account*. He proposed that the survey be funded so the rate could be refined and the family spared a surprise." She set down the pot. "He signed it, which was his error. Professors may calculate anything. They may not calculate *that*, it seems, with a name attached."

"They took it badly."

"They took it as grave-robbery performed with a slide rule. One reviewer coined a phrase that followed him down every corridor in Edinburgh for twenty-seven years." She said it without inflection, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "*The ghoulish arithmetic.* He was a gentle man, Mr. Joiner. He measured ice because it was the largest patient thing in creation and he loved patient things. He answered the reviewers once, one letter, citing the Bossons precedent, and never again."

"Bossons?"

"Mont Blanc. 1820. Three guides taken by an avalanche into the ice; Forbes and others computed the flow; and in 1861, forty-one years on, at the foot of the Glacier des Bossons, the remains came out within a season of the estimate. It is the one experiment of its kind ever completed, and it proved the method entire." She looked at the inked anatomy on the wall. "Proved it for Chamonix. Chamonix rates, Chamonix ice. This glacier is steeper, colder, fed differently; my father's window here was his own, derived from his own line, and the Alpine Journal called it ghoulish, and the funding went elsewhere, and he measured on his own purse, eleven summers out of the next twenty-seven. He re-derived the window four times as the data thickened. It narrowed each time and its center never moved. I can show you the sheets. The center of my father's window, Mr. Joiner, is the season now outside that door."

The room was quiet except for the hotel being a hotel somewhere below us.

"He died in the winter of '93," she said. "In March, at the end, he had the notebooks brought to the bed, which the doctor permitted because the doctor was a fool of the kindest sort, and he put his finger on the last line of the last table and said to me: *someone must be at the bottom when it proves. Not for the Journal. Because a thing this patient deserves a witness, and the boy deserves a churchyard.*" She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, her one gesture of the whole recital. "I lecture arithmetic at a school for girls. I have two months a year and the wrong sex for the Alpine Club, whose members have subscribed, over the years, eleven pounds to this survey, mostly to be rid of my letters. This is my third summer on my father's line. Now you know what I am doing on the moraine, which the village has wondered agreeably about for two years."

"And Fell?"

"Ah," she said. "Confession." She rose and stood at her north window. "Your Mr. Fell came to me on the moraine the last week of July, a year ago. I knew him for the view man; everyone did; the black coat and that mule. He was civil. He asked intelligent questions about the survey, which from a photographer disarmed me, and then he asked the one question, and the asking of it in a stranger's mouth frightened me, if you want the truth. He said: *Madam. At the rates you publish, a burden taken in at the top in July of '65: when does the ice pay it out, and where do I stand?* No preamble. Like a man at a booking office." She turned around. "I told him my figures were not for the picture trade. He looked at me the way a man looks who has been handed back his own sin in the wrong change, thanked me, and went down the moraine, and five weeks later the village said the view man had paid his bill and gone, and I confess I was relieved. I took him for a scavenger with a lens, Mr. Joiner. A quality scavenger. The kind that would sell the arrival."

"And now?"

"Now I have looked into that instrument of his," she said, "and I find he was doing my father's work with tools my father never had, and asking my question with his hat in his hand, and I sent him away, and he built a stone station and did it alone, and wherever he is at this hour he is the one man in Europe who believed my father without being related to him." She came back to the table and sat down with the squared decision of a chairman gaveling herself to order. "So. Terms."

We made them in twenty minutes, and they held, which is more than most treaties manage, and I set them down here as they were spoken.

Her figures and his plates would be put together, hers the rate, his the position, and the sum kept by the two of us and Clemenz and no other soul until it ripened. Nothing to my firm; a catalogue house had no claim on a churchyard. Nothing, on any account, at any price, under any form of words, to Colonel Byron K. Prewitt, whom she pronounced without heat as a fact of the season, like the horseflies. When the window's center approached, a letter would go privately to the family in Scotland, hers to write, she having the language of such letters and I not, offering them silence or presence as they should choose. If the ice delivered, the family's wishes governed everything thereafter: the law, the church, the newspapers told or never told. If it did not deliver, we would strike the tents in October, say nothing, and she would come back with her stakes the next year, and the next, for as long as schools have summers.

"And Fell?" I said, at the door.

"Fell has the only other set of these facts on earth and a stone station on my moraine," said Alexandra Forsyth. "Find him, Mr. Joiner, before the season does. A man who has waited thirty-one years for one photograph will not send out cards announcing where he stands. But he is standing somewhere on my line."

I went down the path to the village in the long gold evening with the folding viewer buttoned inside my coat, and did the day's last professional act at the Monte Rosa desk, which was to hand Herr Imboden the Friday wire for Chicago. ALL PROCEEDS SATISFACTORILY. Forty-one numbers remain. WEATHER FAVORABLE.

It was the first dispatch of my career that was false in every clause, including the weather, which broke that night on a south wind and rained for three days, and I lay listening to it on the roof of Ambrose Fell's room, doing the new arithmetic, which had a window, and a center, and the center was August.
