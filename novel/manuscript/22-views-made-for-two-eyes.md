# 22. Views Made for Two Eyes

There is a shop on Orange Street in Redlands, California, three doors up from the bank whose interior joinery is the best thing about it, the bank's own directors say so, and the gold leaf on the shop's window was laid by the same hand that lettered a door under a pepper tree in Highland thirty years before, the hand being older now and the letters, its owner maintains, better, gold leaf being one of the crafts a man is only fit to be trusted with after sixty. The window says:

H. JOINER
VIEWS & PORTRAITS
*Stereoscopic Work a Specialty*

I ran the firm's western department out of Los Angeles for two years first, and ran it well, and grew rich enough to be dangerous to my own plans, and in the spring of 1899 I resigned it by letter to E. O. Stroud, who wired back NAMES SELL AND NOW I HAVE TAUGHT YOU TO KNOW IT WHAT DOES JOINER WANT, the old question, his life's one question, and this time I had the answer filed and waiting: a contract for two boxed sets a year at western subjects of my own choosing, distribution his, negatives mine. Mine. He fought a month over that clause for the honor of the thing, conceded it in a telegram it must have cost him a doctor's visit to send, and has made money on me every season since, which soothes him.

The shop is small. It smells of walnut shavings and fixer, which is the smell of my whole inheritance in one room, my father having built the counters and the racks and declared the work, on completing it, *adequate premises for a beginner of fifty*. On the counter stands a walnut stereoscope of his making, free to any hand that reaches for it, and in the rack of it, most months, is whatever I judge that month to be the best view in the place. People come in to order wedding portraits and cabinet cards, and while they wait their turn they look into the viewer, and I watch them from the counter, and every so often, three or four times in a season, a customer goes quiet at the eyepiece a certain way, a stillness that begins in the shoulders, and I know the parlor in Highland has acquired another tenant, and I let them stay in there as long as they care to. It is not a sales method. It is a re-investment of capital, at the original rate of interest.

What they are looking into, this season, is *The High Alps, New Series*, one hundred and twenty numbers, Sun Sculpture by A. Fell and H. Joiner, the box lid gilt, the catalogue essay describing, in prose my mother has formally disowned, the eternal snows and incomparable situations. Number 87 is the Matterhorn from the east, morning, with a guide on the moraine for scale, nineteen years old, upright as a fencepost, in a black hat two sizes shy. The set has sold, Stroud's last statement advises me, forty-one thousand boxes, and the sales manager has coined for the joint byline a phrase I am invited to admire, *the two-name guarantee*, so that the names, having been let in the door for commerce, are now kept for commerce, and I find I am at peace with that arrangement, it being the door's whole habit of opening that matters, my own trade having taught me nothing if not that the reason a door first opens is rarely the reason it stays open.

Of the others, the reader will want the ledger closed, and it closes like this, as of this writing.

Clemenz Biner's twelfth plate from the masonry station arrived in March in a case built better than the firm builds them, with a letter, in English improving at compound interest, reporting the winter's ice, the progress of his guide's aspirancy, and the health of the hat. He has made, by my count of his marginal confessions, three portraits of a certain Katharina of the Zermatterhof dining room, and the third one, he writes, *she has permitted to keep*, and I have written back that in my professional judgment, founded on the archives of two continents, no photograph was ever more efficiently employed.

Alexandra Forsyth's letters come eight or nine to the year, in a hand like set type, and are composed of survey data, book recommendations delivered as orders, corrections of my geology, and, buried in each at a depth she calculates precisely, one sentence worth the postage of the whole. The Line Endowment has bought her instruments her father drew in dream margins. Last month's letter came with a printed circular: she is asked to Alaska next summer, a private expedition of scientific men that a railroad magnate is fitting out to survey that coast, glaciers by the hundred mile, unnamed, unmeasured, unphotographed; there is a place in the scientific party; and there is, she is informed, a shortage of operators in America equal to glacier work. The sentence worth the postage, this time, stood alone at the letter's foot, after the signature, in the manner of her nation, which puts the front door at the back of the house:

*The ice there is said to move at rates my father would have called intemperate. Bring the big camera, Mr. Joiner, and your two kinds of eyes.*

The letter is on the desk before me now, one month unanswered, which Gina at the bank, who handles my accounts and my excuses, says is one month exactly longer than I have ever let Stroud wait. Beside it lies a steamship folder for the Seattle boats, which arrived in the shop by some means I have not investigated, my mother having visited Tuesday.

Of Ambrose Fell I have had, in two years, two communications. The first came at Christmas of '97 from Vernazza, a print, no letter: the harbor in winter light, the boats hauled out, the whole town's washing strung across the street like signal flags, and on the back, in pencil, *the trunk is settled. A.F.* The second came last October with an Egyptian stamp, a card of the excursion-steamer landing at Luxor, the message in full: *Wash water here undrinkable and undiscussable. The light, however. — A.F.* No address given, either time. Considine writes that a small house in London has lately issued a folio of Ligurian studies, *signed in the corner, Joiner, in pencil, grain of rice, you'd need a loupe and I had one*, and that the trade cannot discover the operator, and that the plates are, he is prepared to state professionally, the finest sustained photography of the age. I have written Considine that I concur in the attribution and decline to assist the discovery. Some men spend thirty years learning to sign their work. It seems right that the world be made to spend a few learning to read the signature.

The Vernazza print, the one the Signora tied up in newspaper, the door half open at the end of the whitewashed corridor and the woman's shadow on the floor of the lit room beyond, folding, content, hangs in the shop, behind the counter, where I see it and customers mostly don't. I never learned who she was, and the older I get the less I believe that was ever the question the picture was asking. My mother looked at it a long while her first visit, and did not ask who, and asked instead where the light was coming from, the true question of her son's whole trade, and the answer, which pleased her, is: from the room ahead.

And on most fine evenings, at closing, a yellow dog of the neighborhood, whose nominal owner is the druggist and whose actual arrangements are her own, comes up Orange Street on her rounds and lies down across my doorway in the last sun, and will not be argued with, there being no arguing with an animal who has correctly identified the warmest stone in the town and the softest touch behind it, and I have taken to timing the day's last task to her arrival, which is the lettering of caption cards for the spring set, *California: The Mountains and the Sea*, in drawing ink, in my best hand, at the counter my father built.

I was at that work an evening ago, the dog at the door, the town going quiet, when a family came in late off the electric car, a citrus man and his wife and a boy of nine or ten who had been promised, evidently, one look in the walnut viewer as the treat at the end of an errand-day, and while his parents chose a frame the boy stood up on the stool and put his face to the hood, and went still in the shoulders, and stayed.

Number 87 was in the rack.

I did not interrupt, having been raised in the trade. I lettered my cards, and the light came long down Orange Street as it comes in the last hour, gold over the groves as it was gold over the groves when a train took me east through the blossom smell half a life ago, and after a while the boy said, without lifting his face from the eyepiece, to the room in general, in the voice they always use, the ones the card is going to keep:

"Who's the man standing on the rocks?"

I laid the day's last caption flat to dry, and came around the counter to tell him.
