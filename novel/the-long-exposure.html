1. Sun Sculpture

The Hotel del Coronado photographed best at seven in the morning, before the sea wind came up and before the guests came down to stand in front of things. I had learned this over four days of trying. The hotel was a great white heap of shingle and turret, roofed in red, built all of wood by men who must have believed fire was something that happened to other people, and at seven the light came across the bay low and warm and picked out every rail and bracket of it, so that the whole absurd wedding cake of a building looked carved rather than carpentered.

I was twenty-six that spring, and I had spent three years of my life making pictures of things for people who would never go and see them. That was the business. Stroud Brothers of Chicago published stereoscopic views, boxed sets of them, one hundred cards to a set, and sold them door to door across the whole of the United States by an army of college boys on commission. A card is a simple thing to describe. Two photographs of the same subject, made side by side through two lenses set as far apart as a pair of human eyes, mounted together on a curve of gray board. Looked at flat, they are twins, and dull ones. Looked at through the stereoscope, the two views pour together and stand up off the card, and the parlor wall falls away, and a person in Dubuque who will never in this life hold a steamship ticket is standing on the rim of the Grand Canyon with the morning cold coming up out of it.

The catalogue called our views Sun Sculptures. That was Mr. Stroud's phrase, and he was proud of it, and for once the salesmanship was honest. A flat photograph is a picture of a thing. A stereograph is the thing, or near enough that your body believes it a little, which is a different article entirely and worth a dollar a dozen of anybody's money.

That morning I was making negative number nine of a set to be called California: The Golden Shore, and the hotel's assistant manager for publicity, a Mr. Loper, was standing at my elbow ruining it.

"You'll want the flag in," said Mr. Loper.

"The flag is in."

"Sharp, I mean. Snapping. Can you have it snapping?"

"I can have it snapping or I can have it sharp," I said. "The wind moves it and the plate takes its time. Which would you rather?"

He thought about this seriously, as though I had offered him a choice of entrées. Mr. Loper was a young man with a mustache he had grown to seem older and a straw hat he wore to seem gayer, and both articles worked against each other. "Sharp," he said at last. "A blurred flag looks like weather coming."

"There's no weather coming."

"Looks like it, though. In the picture. People study these things through the glass, Mr. Joiner, they see everything." He was right about that, at least. Through the lens of a stereoscope a customer sees more of a place than most visitors ever do standing in it. I have had letters forwarded from the firm, care of the caption department, asking after the name of a dog asleep on a porch in negative number forty-one, set eleven, Old Mexico.

Mr. Loper's other request that week had been the tent city. South of the hotel, along the strand, there stood several acres of white canvas where families who could not afford the Del's tariff boarded by the week, bathed in the same ocean, and by every report had considerably more fun. The hotel wished the tents kept out of the views. I had kept them out. It was not my business what a hotel wished to admit about itself, and anyway the tents would get their own card someday, in some cheaper set, and be loved by exactly the people who had stayed in them.

I made the exposure at ten past seven. The flag, for the record of anyone studying the matter through a lens, is sharp.

I should say what I looked like standing there, since it bears on certain furniture of this story, though less than you might suppose and less than it bore on where I slept. I am tall, and I am colored, as the word went then, my father being a black man of Tennessee and my mother a white woman of Ohio, and what the mixture had produced was a man brown enough that hotels solved the problem of me in a variety of ways, each presented as a courtesy. The Del's particular courtesy was a clean, narrow room in the staff annex, back of the laundry, while the firm was billed for accommodations at the guest rate. I noted the difference in my expense book, in the column I kept for such arithmetic, which I had headed years before, in a mood I no longer entirely remembered, ITEM: AMERICA.

The annex had one advantage, and that was breakfast. The waiters ate before service, at a long table by the kitchen, and they ate what the chef was proudest of and not what moved slowest from the larder, and after four mornings among them I had been adopted outright. A waiter named Rutherford, who had come out from New Orleans by way of a Pullman car, took a proprietary interest in my equipment and my prospects. He was sixty and had seen everything twice.

"What they pay you to make a picture of this hotel?" he asked me, the last morning.

"They pay me by the month. The hotel doesn't pay me anything. The hotel is a subject."

"A subject." He liked that. He looked out the annex door at the great red roof of the thing, turning the word over. "And these pictures sell in Chicago."

"Chicago, Cincinnati, Boston. Anywhere there's a parlor and a door to knock on."

"People in Boston buying pictures of a hotel they never stayed at, never will."

"That's the whole trade," I said. "You put the world in a box. The box goes on the parlor table. Sunday afternoon, the family passes the viewer around, and everybody travels. It's cheaper than the railroad and nobody gets seasick."

Rutherford considered this and poured me more coffee. "White folks," he said, "will buy anything flat and call it someplace." Which remains, of all the descriptions of the photographic industry I have heard in fifty years, the one requiring the fewest corrections.

The telegram found me at four o'clock that afternoon on the north veranda, cleaning plate holders. A boy in hotel livery carried it out on an actual silver tray, which tells you what the Del thought of telegrams, and stood by while I read it, in case there should be a reply, or a tip, or a scene.

It was from Chicago, from the office of Mr. E. O. Stroud himself, and it ran this way:

FELL SILENT SINCE AUGUST STOP ALPS SERIES INCOMPLETE STOP FINISH GOLDEN SHORE AND COME CHICAGO FIRST TRAIN STOP SAY NOTHING STOP E O STROUD

I read it twice and gave the boy a dime to disappoint him honestly.

Everyone in the trade knew Fell, and no one in America knew him. That is a riddle with a plain answer. The finest views the firm had ever issued, the sets that had built the Stroud name and half the Stroud building, went out into the world under the imprint alone. The High Alps. The Holy Land. Egypt and the Nile. The catalogue did not say who had stood behind the camera, because the catalogue's theory was that the firm was the artist, as a mill might claim to be the weaver. The man who had stood behind it, for most of thirty years and on four continents, was called Ambrose Fell. Operators spoke of him as apprentice masons speak of the man who set the keystone in the bridge they cross to work. I had been raised, in a manner of speaking, inside his pictures. There had been a stereoscope in my mother's parlor in Highland, California, and a set of the original High Alps got secondhand from a Redlands estate sale, and before I ever saw a mountain with snow on it I had stood on a dozen of them in a walnut viewer with my supper going cold. Number 87 of that set was the Matterhorn from the east, with a black-hatted guide standing on the moraine for scale, and I can tell you now, as a professional matter, that the guide stands exactly where he must stand for the ice to read as ice and the mountain to leap back four miles behind him. At nine years old I did not know why the card worked. I only knew that when I looked into it something in my chest went quiet.

Silent since August. It was March. A man does not go quiet for seven months on the strength of bad mails.

I sat with the telegram a while, and the afternoon went on around me in its expensive way. Croquet on the south lawn. A naval officer explaining the fleet to two women who were watching a third woman instead. The Pacific laying itself down on the strand over and over with that sound that is not quite applause. I thought about what SAY NOTHING meant coming from a man who owned a caption department, and I thought about seven months, and I confess that under the worry, where I could pretend not to see it, there was a bright vein of something that was not worry at all. The Alps series. Incomplete.

Mr. Loper came by at five to ask whether I might, before departing, make a view of the new electric launch, the hotel having lately electrified everything that would hold still for it.

"I'm called back to Chicago," I told him. "Urgent business of the firm."

"Nothing wrong, I hope."

"A man forgot to write home," I said. "It happens in the best families."

The light was still good, so I finished the plate holders, and made the launch for him anyway at six, with the water going pewter and the hotel behind it holding the last of the sun the way white wood does, gently, as if it had been asked to hold it for a friend who was coming right back. It was a good negative. It went out that autumn as number twelve of the set, and if you have the card, and a glass, you may observe at the launch's stern a man in a straw hat, blurred, waving at the camera against express instructions.

I packed my cases that night and caught the morning ferry, and the Del stood on the water behind me with its flags snapping, unphotographably sharp.

2. The Joiner Shop

The eastbound out of Chicago would not leave San Bernardino without me until Thursday, which gave me a day and two nights at home, and home was Highland, eight miles up the valley, where the orange groves run out against the first rock of the mountains and stop as sharply as if a surveyor had drawn the line between two kinds of ambition.

My father's shop stood behind the house, under a pepper tree, with JOINER lettered on the door in gold leaf he had laid himself. The sign was older than I was and it said everything the family owned worth saying. My father had been born with no surname a court would recognize, in Tennessee, in 1845, as another man's property. He had walked off that property at seventeen, been a teamster for the Union, then a soldier of the 55th Massachusetts, and after the war he had stayed in Boston and apprenticed himself to a firm of finish carpenters, and when at last a clerk in a records office asked him what name he meant to carry, he gave the man his trade. He liked to say he had considered Cabinetmaker and rejected it as putting on airs.

In Boston he had married my mother, which was legal there and illegal in most other places the two of them might have thought to live, my mother being white, a schoolteacher out of Ohio with a spine you could have squared lumber against. They came west in 1870 on the theory, my mother's, that a new country would have less time to mind other people's business. The theory proved optimistic in the usual ways. Still, the valley needed finish work more than it needed grief. The men putting up mansion after mansion down in Redlands on orange money wanted mantels and paneling and stair rails of a quality that did not care who supplied it, and my father's prices were fair and his miters were shut tight as scripture, and by the time I was old enough to sweep shavings the shop had a waiting list and the family had a piano.

I got down from the local at the Highland depot Wednesday noon with two equipment cases and a valise, and found my father in the shop gluing up a tabletop, and stood in the doorway a moment before he saw me, because a man at glue-up cannot be interrupted and both of us would have been embarrassed by how glad we were.

"Take the far clamp," he said, when he saw me. So I set my cases down and took the far clamp, and we worked the panel true together, and only when the last screw was snugged did he straighten up and look at me properly. "They feeding you, out at that hotel?"

"The waiters were. The dining room had reservations."

He grunted at the joke, which was his highest award for one, and wiped his hands. "Your mother's at the school till three. There's ham."

Over the ham I told him about Chicago, as much as SAY NOTHING allowed, which I decided meant everything, since the instruction had plainly been drafted with stockholders in mind and not fathers. I told him Fell had gone silent in Switzerland with the firm's greatest property half made, and that I was being sent east to be told why, and that I believed, from the shape of the telegram, that after Chicago there would be a steamship in it.

My father ate for a while.

"That's the man made your mountain," he said at last.

"Number 87. Yes."

"Never did put his name on it."

"The firm doesn't put names on anything. The firm is the artist."

"Mm," said my father, who had signed the underside of every drawer he had built since 1866. He got up and poured coffee for us both, and stood with his back against the sink in a manner I knew meant a speech was assembling, a speech being for him anything upward of three sentences. "When a man leaves work half done," he said, "it's the work quit him, or he quit the work, or somebody quit him of it. You'll want to know which, before you stand in his place. Men get strange about the last of a thing." He looked into his coffee. "Especially the good ones."

I have been paid for advice many times since, and given it, and I have never heard the business of that summer set out more completely than my father set it out before I had so much as bought my ticket. I did not understand this until later, which is the natural fate of advice.

My mother came home at three with chalk on her cuff and a composition book under her arm, kissed me, held me off at arm's length to conduct her inspection, and said, "You've been letting hotels feed you in the kitchen."

"Only the better hotels."

"Hollis."

"It's a long fight, Ma, and I had plates to make."

She let it go, in the sense that she folded it and put it away in the drawer where she kept things that were not finished being fought about. My mother had spent thirty years teaching the valley's children grammar, and the campaign had left her with an exactness that made her, among other things, the best caption critic I ever had. That evening I showed my parents the proofs of the Golden Shore set, passing the cards one at a time into the walnut stereoscope on the parlor table, the same instrument, its hood rebuilt by my father in '79 after the original split, through which I had first seen Venice, and the pyramids, and Number 87. My mother read each proposed caption aloud the way a judge reads an indictment.

"'The world-famed Hotel del Coronado, whose incomparable situation,'" she read, and stopped. "Whose. The situation doesn't belong to the hotel, the hotel is in the situation."

"The caption department is very attached to 'whose.'"

"The caption department," she said, "was raised by wolves."

My father, at the viewer, had gone slow and quiet in the manner of a man actually looking, and he stayed a long time on number nine, the hotel at seven in the morning with the light coming low across the bay. When he set it down he said, "That's good glasswork at the rail there," meaning the turned balusters of the veranda, in deep relief in the near plane, each one round as a thing you could close your hand on. Other men praised my skies. My father praised my balusters. Between the two I built my whole idea of what a view owes the person holding it.

There is one more thing from that night worth setting down. When the cards were put away my mother stayed by the lamp, and I could see her working up to a subject, and I supposed it would be the ocean crossing, or my clothes for it.

"You'll be over there among strangers a long while, by the sound of it," she said. "I'll want letters. Real ones. Not weather."

"You'll have them."

"And Hollis." She looked at me over the top of her spectacles, a gesture she performed with the authority of long service. "Over there, I'm told, they will mostly not know what to make of you. A great many of them have never seen an American of any description. You'll be a curiosity before you're anything else."

"I'm a curiosity here, Ma."

"Here," she said, "you are a Joiner," and she tapped the table once, as though entering the fact in a ledger, and took up her composition books, and that was the end of the sitting.

Thursday morning my father drove me down to the San Bernardino depot behind the bay mare, through the groves, with the mountains standing up hard on our left in their spring snow. I had grown up under those mountains and had photographed them perhaps two hundred times, and I want to record honestly what I felt looking at them that morning, with a ticket to Chicago in my breast pocket and beyond it, unpurchased and shining, a ticket to the Alps. I felt disloyal. It is a small, absurd feeling and I have since learned that every traveler owns a version of it, but there it was, riding along between us in the buggy. San Gorgonio hung white over the valley like a debt.

At the depot my father carried the heavier case to the platform himself, which the porter permitted with professional sympathy, one tradesman to another. The California Limited came down out of the pass on time, brakes singing, all varnish and importance.

"Mind the good ones," my father said. It had cost him the whole drive to compress the speech to that.

"I will."

He shook my hand as if we were concluding a sale, and then, with the handshake still going, put his left hand on my shoulder, which in the full vocabulary of Ezra Joiner was an embrace with weeping. I boarded, found my seat among some cattlemen and a honeymoon couple, and put my face to the glass. He was already walking back along the platform, not turning, one hand up in the air behind him in farewell, a man with glue drying at home.

The train ran east through the groves, and the whole car filled with the smell of orange blossom, which is a smell I have met since on three continents, in soaps and tonics and once in a cathedral, and which has never once meant anything but this: the last two miles of the San Bernardino Valley, going away.

Then Cajon Pass took us, and the desert had the train the rest of the day.

3. The Planet Earth, Delivered

The Stroud Brothers building stood on Wabash Avenue, six stories of brick and confidence, and it worked on the principle of a packing house, except that what went in one end was the visible world and what came out the other was cardboard, in cartons, freight prepaid.

I had been in the building twice before, both times briefly, and I had never gotten over it, and I never have. On the second floor, forty women at forty benches mounted paired prints on curved card stock at a rate the foreman posted daily on a chalkboard, as though depth itself were a commodity with a market price, which at Stroud Brothers it was. On the third floor the caption department composed, in six-point type, the sentences my mother held in such low esteem. On the fourth, behind a fireproof door, was the negative vault, and if the reader wants to know what the trade felt like from inside in those years I can do no better than that room: eleven hundred wooden cabinets in ranks under electric light, every drawer packed edgewise with glass, every pane of glass a place on the earth, the whole of it cool and smelling faintly of collodion and cedar, and quiet, with the quiet of a bank rather than a church, though I have been in churches that had less claim to the comparison.

Passing the second-floor landing I heard, through a frosted door, the voice of the sales manager instructing that season's crop of college-boy canvassers. "You are not selling cardboard, gentlemen," the voice was saying, with the weariness of a man who says it every March. "You are selling the planet Earth, delivered."

Mr. E. O. Stroud received me in a corner office hung with framed testimonials and one enormous photograph of the firm's Kansas City warehouse, which told you where his heart kept its accounts. He was sixty, square, pink, dressed like a bank and barbered like a senator, and he came around the desk to shake hands, which he did not do for everyone, and I record it because it was the first of perhaps five occasions in my life on which I watched a man decide, visibly, in the very motion of rising, that the profitable course was to have no opinion about my complexion. Commerce is not virtue. It is only sometimes wearing its clothes.

"Joiner," he said. "The Coronado proofs came up this morning. The balustrade study is the best card in the set."

"Thank you, sir."

"Sit down. You know why you're here?"

"The telegram was economical."

"Telegrams cost by the word and so do rumors." He sat, laced his fingers over his waistcoat, and looked at me for a while in silence, and I understood that whatever came next had been rehearsed, and worried over, and that Mr. E. O. Stroud, who owned eleven hundred cabinets of the visible world, was frightened of some part of what he was about to say.

"Tell me first," he said, "what you know about the High Alps series."

"Issued 1879. One hundred and twenty numbers. It's the set that made the house. It has been reprinted so many times the negatives are failing, and the newer houses are shooting the same country with fresh plates and better emulsions, and their cards are cleaner than our reprints. Two years ago the firm decided to re-make the entire set, number for number. There was one man it could belong to."

"You've been listening at doors."

"No, sir. I've been in the trade. The trade has no other subject."

He almost smiled and it went out like a match. "Ambrose Fell left for Switzerland in April of last year," he said. "The plan was the full season, April to October, one hundred and twenty numbers, the old compositions re-made exactly. Fell shot the originals in '78. Nobody living knows those stations better." He paused a moment on the word living as if he had caught himself walking on ice too early in the year. "Plates came through Basle by freight all spring and summer. Manifests regular as rent. Then August." He opened a drawer and set a thin folder in front of me, and I want to describe that folder honestly, because I would spend the next five months of my life inside it. It held three shipping manifests, a hotel bill, and one letter.

The manifests were in Fell's hand, which I knew from the vault, a narrow upright hand, plate numbers and subjects in columns. The first two were what they should have been. Groups of numbers, subjects to match the shot list. No. 63, The Gorner Glacier from the Riffelhorn. No. 71, Zermatt Village and Church. The third manifest, dated the 9th of August, 1896, listed eleven plates, and the subject column, for all eleven, said only: The Matterhorn Glacier, from the moraine at Zmutt.

"Eleven plates of one subject," I said.

"That's a man straightening out his inventory, maybe. Duplicate stations. Weather trials." He said it as though repeating something he had paid a subordinate to tell him.

"Is the subject on the shot list?"

"The shot list wants the Matterhorn from Zmutt. One number." Mr. Stroud looked out his window at Wabash Avenue. "There's no station on that list wants the glacier. Ice in the middle ground, that's all it's rated. You can't sell a customer a hundred yards of dirty ice, Joiner. There's no depth in it and no romance."

The hotel bill was from the Monte Rosa Hotel, Zermatt, the house of the Seiler family, addressed to the firm with Alpine courtesy and an unmistakable Alpine firmness, for a room held vacant, with effects, since the 12th of September, 1896.

The letter was Fell's. It was dated the 30th of August and it was four sentences long, and I will set it down in full because I came to know it as men know psalms.

Sirs. The season's numbers to date are sixty-one, of which forty-three are equal or superior to the originals and the rest will want re-making. I am pursuing a subject of importance not on the list, of which more when it can be shown rather than argued. You will forgive the delay or you will not; the subject will not wait on either. — A.F.

I read it three times. "And after this, nothing."

"Nothing. Letters to the hotel come back unopened. The Seilers write that he paid his own bill through August, took two cases of equipment and a mule, and quit the valley. A guide brought the mule back. Fell is not in Zermatt, or if he is, no one who answers mail knows it." Stroud took the folder back, squared its edges, and delivered what he had rehearsed. "I am not sending you to drag the Rhône for a body, Joiner. If Fell is dead the Swiss will find him, they are the tidiest nation on earth, they will invoice us for him. I'm sending you to save the property. The season opens now. You will go to Zermatt as the firm's operator, you will finish the one hundred twenty numbers, and you will find, recover, and inventory every Stroud negative in the Valais, because under his contract every plate that man exposes belongs to this house, and there are fifty-nine numbers loose in those mountains, and some of them, knowing him, are the finest things he ever made."

"And Fell himself?"

"Is a grown man of fifty-six years and my friend of thirty," said Stroud, and for a moment the office furniture of his voice was moved out of it, and I saw the frightened part plainly. Then the furniture came back in. "If you should meet him, you will extend to him every courtesy of the firm, and you will telegraph me the same hour."

There was a silence in which I was evidently meant to say that I understood, so I said something else instead.

"Whose name goes on the new set?"

"What?"

"The re-made High Alps. One hundred and twenty numbers. Whose name?"

"The firm's name," said Stroud, with the genuine bafflement of a man asked whose name goes on the water works. "Same as the originals. Same as your balusters, Joiner, if it comes to that." He studied me a moment, shrewd again. "Did Fell put you up to that question? He's never met you."

"No, sir. It's a question the trade asks itself."

"The trade should ask itself who meets the payroll." He stood; the interview was closing its ledgers. "You sail Saturday from New York, the Fürst Bismarck, second cabin. Considine in London has your credit arranged and your plates, forty dozen, the new emulsion. He will also talk your ear off about Fell; you may keep whatever is useful. Wire me from London, from Lucerne, and from Zermatt, and thereafter every Friday. The code word for Fell in all traffic is MERCHANDISE."

I stood too. At the door his voice caught me once more.

"Joiner. The eleven plates on that last manifest never shipped. They're not in Basle and they're not in this building. Whatever he was pursuing, he kept it." Mr. Stroud had his back to the window, so that I could not read his face against the light, an arrangement I did not believe accidental. "Sixty-one from one hundred twenty leaves you fifty-nine numbers, a strange country, and half a season to make them in. Most operators would fail. I'm told you studied the originals as a boy."

"I could draw you Number 87 from memory."

"Don't draw it," he said. "Go stand where he stood."

I had thirty hours in Chicago and spent ten of them in the negative vault with the 1878 High Alps, drawer by drawer, against the light of a sorting table, and I will not pretend that was duty. It was the nearest thing my trade owned to reading a master's workbook. On the glass, in the corners, outside the mount line where no customer would ever see, Fell had scratched his private marks. Station notes. Times of day. Once, on Number 87, my Matterhorn, in letters I needed a loupe to find, two words that were either a joke or a confession or a craftsman's plain memorandum, and I copied them into my own notebook without deciding which.

Too soon.

4. The Wide Gray Yard

The Fürst Bismarck took eight days to Southampton, and I passed them in the manner of second cabin, which is to say among people who could afford to cross the ocean but had been made to feel they should apologize for the way they did it, and were therefore friendlier than the people upstairs.

On the first afternoon out a gentleman from Rochester in a plaid ulster handed me his coat and hat at the door of the smoking room. I took them, considered the coat, judged the cloth, and handed them back.

"Decent wool," I said, "but the shoulders are cut too generous for you. Whoever sold it saw you coming."

There is a particular silence a man falls into while he re-sorts his entire understanding of a situation, and I had long since learned to wait through it comfortably, having caused a great deal of it in my time. The gentleman from Rochester came out the far side of the silence red as flannel, apologized more than was needed, and spent the remaining week overcorrecting, so that by the Fastnet light I knew the names of his three daughters and his opinion of free silver, and had twice declined the loan of the ulster against the deck wind. People are mostly not wicked. They are lazy, and they have bought their ideas the way he bought the coat, ready made, without asking about the shoulders.

The Germans of the crew, for their part, had settled the question of me at the gangway with one word, Amerikaner, filed under it whatever else they noticed, and moved on to matters of importance, such as whether the wind would let the tables be set with the good glasses. I found I liked being a nation instead of a problem. It was restful, in the way of any vacation, and like any vacation it would end at a border.

Mornings I walked the deck. Afternoons I worked. I had the shot list to learn, one hundred and twenty numbers with Fell's original station notes copied out beside sixty-one check marks, and I had a book, bought at a shop on lower Broadway the morning we sailed, called Scrambles Amongst the Alps, by Mr. Edward Whymper.

I record here only what a man could learn on a steamer's deck chair, because that was all I then knew, and a story ought to be honest about the order in which it learned things. Mr. Whymper was an engraver of London who in the 1860s had made himself the most determined mountaineer alive, and the great prize of that decade was the Matterhorn, which had turned back every party that tried it and had gathered about itself the reputation of a thing not meant to be had. In July of 1865 Whymper reached the top with six companions. It is the most famous mountain ascent ever made, and the reason it is famous is what happened on the way down. A rope broke. Four men went down the north face, a fall you may measure in thousands of feet: a Chamonix guide named Michel Croz, a clergyman named Hudson, a young man named Hadow, and a lord of nineteen named Francis Douglas. Whymper and two Zermatt guides, a father and son both named Peter Taugwalder, survived on the upper end of the broken rope.

The bodies were recovered from the glacier below some days after. Three of them were.

I read that chapter twice, the second time in that state of settled dread with which one reads a thing that has already happened and cannot be prevented by any amount of attention, and then I sat with the book shut on my knee and the wide gray yard of the Atlantic going by, and I thought about Number 87 in my mother's parlor, the mountain leaping back four miles behind the black-hatted guide, and how I had stood inside that card a hundred times at nine years old and had known nothing at all about what the ice at the bottom of it was holding.

The fourth man was never found. Whymper gives it one sentence, and shuts the sentence like a door.

⁂

The firm's London office was in High Holborn, a shopfront with a viewing parlor where the public might sample the merchandise in mahogany stereoscopes chained to the counter like city bibles, and a floor above it where Mr. Considine conducted the correspondence of three continents from within a weather system of paper entirely his own.

"Joiner, is it, Joiner, yes," he said, shaking my hand with both of his, moving a chair's cargo of catalogues to the floor for me. "The Coronado man. Lovely rail work in that number nine, lovely, the girls in mounting fought over it." He was fifty or so, Irish, collarless by four in the afternoon, and he talked as rain falls in his country, steadily, warmly, with no evident plan of stopping. I had been instructed I might keep whatever was useful. I kept nearly all of it.

The plates were cased and waiting, forty dozen, the new emulsion, and a letter of credit on a bank in Lausanne, and my rail bookings, Charing Cross to Paris to Lucerne. Business done, Considine took me down to the viewing parlor, locked the till out of ceremony rather than distrust, and stood me in front of the window display, where between a pyramid of Egypt and the Nile and a fan of The Holy Land sat the old High Alps box, its gilt gone tender at the corners.

"Now," he said. "You'll want Fell."

"Mr. Stroud said you'd talk."

"Mr. Stroud pays me partly for what I know and mostly so he needn't hear it himself." He took the Matterhorn card out of the box, Number 87, and dropped it in the rack of the nearest stereoscope, and motioned me to the eyepiece while he talked, so that the whole account came to me with the mountain standing in front of it, which I have always suspected he did on purpose. Considine was a salesman before he was a correspondent, and he knew a lesson wants a picture.

The account, shorn of its weather, ran so. Ambrose Fell had come to the firm in 1870, out of a failed portrait studio in Manchester, already the best technical man anyone had seen. In 1878 the brothers sent him to Switzerland for the set that would make everybody's fortune but his. He was nine months in the high country with a mule, a tent of rubbered canvas, and a boy from Chamonix to carry glass. The contract he had signed before sailing gave the firm, in language, said Considine, that would strip paint at forty years' distance, every negative, every right, every mention. Fell had signed it because he was thirty-eight years old and broke, which is the age and condition at which the trade gets everyone's signature.

"He never forgave the contract," I said.

"He never forgave himself," said Considine. "The contract he understood. Firms are firms the way weather is weather; you don't forgive a wet April, you buy a coat. No. What ate him was that he agreed. He sold his name cheerful as a man selling a horse he thinks is spavined, and then had to watch the horse win the Derby for twenty years running." He rapped the stereoscope with a knuckle. "That card there has been in a million parlors. A million, Joiner, I've seen the ledgers. Name one soul outside this trade who's heard of Ambrose Fell."

Through the eyepiece the mountain stood in its four miles of distance, and on the moraine the black-hatted guide waited where he had waited since 1878, the size of a beetle in creation, and I thought, not for the first time since the vault, that I did not know who the guide was either.

In 1889 Fell had gone as far as the door. A rival house, one of the new combinations, offered him his own imprint, his name in gold on the box lid. He had let them draw the contract, and then in the end had not signed, and no one knew why, though Considine had his theory, delivered at a lower pressure than the rest of him, almost gently.

"Marianne wouldn't have it. His wife. She had a horror of grand gestures, that woman, she'd seen what they cost. She told him a name on a box is still a box, and a quarrel dressed as a business is still a quarrel, and they'd only ever been happy when he was working and she was mending his socks in eleven countries. So he stayed. And they went on. It wasn't a sad life, mind. I'll not have you thinking it was a sad life. There was no man better company in Europe on his day."

"And then."

"And then she died. Winter of '94, in lodgings in Nice, of her heart, with no warning at all." Considine took the card out of the rack and squared it back into the box with the others. "He worked straight through it, which frightened me worse than drink would have. And when the brothers proposed the great re-making of the Alps set, the greatest compliment the house ever paid a living man, mind, though naturally they'd have printed the water company's name on it sooner than his, Ambrose wrote back one line. I saw the letter. 'Yes. I began there.'" He shut the box lid. "I began there. A man of fifty-six says that about a place, you're not sending an operator, Joiner. You're posting a letter to 1878."

The shop bell rang. Two ladies came in out of the Holborn drizzle to ask for views of the Italian lakes, and Considine went to them all warmth and Como, and I stood a while by the window among the boxed continents.

My boat train left in the morning. He came to the station anyway, talking, carried my case to the platform, and handed it up to me with the one piece of it all he had rehearsed, I think, as against the rest that merely rained.

"If you find him," said Considine, "don't stand between him and the work. Whatever it is. Stand behind the work and look useful. It's the only door into that man, and Joiner, listen to me, it's the only door out."

5. The Playground of Europe

Of Paris I saw eleven hours, most of them freight-related, and I will not embarrass either of us by describing a city on that acquaintance. I ate a supper near the Gare de l'Est that I still think about, and a porter and I conducted a friendship of great warmth and no shared language over the transfer of nine cases of glass, and in the morning France went by the window all day like a well-run farm.

Switzerland announced itself in the customs shed at Basle, where an official examined my forty dozen plates, my letter of credit, my passport, and me, in ascending order of doubt, and finally produced a form which settled everything. There is no situation so irregular that the Swiss cannot regularize it with a form. It is their genius, and I mean that without mockery. A country made of four languages and three religions and one mountain range has to run on something, and they had sensibly chosen paper over blood, which is more than my own country had managed in my lifetime.

I came into Lucerne at evening. The shot list gave me nine numbers there and on the Rigi, the resort mountain that stands over the lake, and I will use the Rigi to explain the trade I was in, because nothing explains it better.

The Rigi is climbed by cog railway. At the summit stand two hotels. At half past three each morning, a porter walks the corridors of those hotels sounding an alp-horn, a sound of great antiquity and no mercy, and several hundred tourists of all nations rise from their beds, wrap themselves in the hotel's blankets, and shuffle out onto the summit lawn in the dark, where they stand like the raised dead in a painting of the Judgment, facing east. In due course the sun arrives. It is, I am bound to report, magnificent: the lake far under its fog, the whole Oberland standing up rose-colored out of the night, a hundred miles of snow lit like the first morning of the world. The raised dead applaud, as though the management had arranged it, and go in to breakfast.

My work was to bottle that. Number 12 of the set: Sunrise from the Rigi-Kulm. Fell's original station note, copied in my book, read: East terrace, third boundary stone, camera height 52 in., wait for the fog to find the lake again. The crowd is the subject; the sun is the lamp. I climbed up the day before, found the third boundary stone with my boot in the dark, set the tripod at fifty-two inches, and when the light came I understood the note, the whole art of it, in one held breath. From that stone, at that height, the crowd on the lawn stood between the camera and the gulf of the lake, every blanketed shoulder rimmed in rose, and the abyss beyond them full of fog just catching fire, and in the stereoscope, I knew, the eye would go from wool you could touch to a hundred miles of morning, and the card would say what no painting of the Judgment ever managed: that the raised dead are ordinary people, cold in their blankets, and that this is the whole poignance of them.

He had solved it in 1878 and left the solution scratched in a corner of the glass for whoever came after. I made three exposures, and packed up, and found that I had, without deciding to, taken off my hat.

The tourists went in to their rolls and coffee. One of them, an Englishwoman of the ringing sort, paused to inspect my camera and asked what I found to photograph up here once the sunrise was finished.

"The boundary stones," I told her, and she went away satisfied, having long suspected the Americans of some such thing.

Nine numbers in eleven days, and then the Valais. The railway goes up the Rhône to Visp, where in those years you changed to the narrow-gauge line for Zermatt, three hours up a valley that closes on you like a hand. Past Stalden the vines quit. Past St. Niklaus the valley walls go up gray and serious and wet with the spring melt, streaked silver, and the river below the trestles runs the color of milk and slate, which is the color of ground-up mountain, the glaciers upstream milling the country to flour a little faster than the country can be rebuilt. Somewhere past Randa I became aware that the German family sharing my carriage had stopped talking, and the silence had that particular upholstered quality of people not discussing a fellow passenger, and I mention it because of what came next, which is the reason I tell this part at all.

At Täsch the eldest boy of the family, who was perhaps eight, lost the battle he had been fighting with his curiosity since Visp, crossed the carriage, planted himself before me, and asked me something in German, gravely, twice. His mother arrived in a storm of apology. I asked her, in French, what the question had been, and she colored and said the boy wished to know whether I was Winnetou, who I later learned is an Indian chief in novels that Germans read as we read dime westerns, the noblest man in all their imagined America. The whole carriage waited on my answer, five faces of five ages, and it was such an improvement on the questions America generally sent to my seat that I gave it the consideration it deserved.

"Tell him no," I said, "but tell him Winnetou and I are countrymen, and he has the advantage of me, because the boy has read those books and I never did."

This was translated. The boy received it with the gravity of state business, shook my hand, and returned to his window, satisfied, and his mother smiled at me for the first time in three hours of shared carriage, and the valley outside was suddenly less gray. I set this down exactly because I promised my mother letters that were not weather, and that was the first thing I wrote her from Zermatt. Curiosity is not kindness. It is only the unlocked door kindness sometimes comes through, when it comes at all, and a traveler learns to work with the doors he finds.

Then the train came around the shoulder at Herbriggen or thereabouts, and the carriage said one word in three languages, and I looked up from my notebook, and there it was.

I want to be careful here. I had known that mountain for seventeen years. I had studied it through a lens since before my voice broke; I could have drawn its silhouette blind, the leaning summit, the shadowed north wall, the whole impossible tusk of it. I was, you might say, the one traveler in that carriage armored against the sight. I record therefore as a plain professional finding that the armor was worth nothing. The Matterhorn through glass is a composition. The Matterhorn through air has mass, and the mass does something at the back of the neck that no card can carry, which was an unwelcome discovery for a man whose living was cards, and I sat back down, having at some point stood, and made myself look at the boy looking at it instead, and that was better. That I could have photographed.

Zermatt in the first week of June was a village getting into its costume. Half the hotels still had their shutters up; the other half were being aired, whitewashed, and provisioned by mule train from the railhead. The season proper would not open till July, and the street belonged to the residents: guides re-splicing rope in doorways, cows going up through town to the high pastures with their bells filling the street wall to wall, English clergymen arriving in twos like migratory birds ahead of their flocks.

The Monte Rosa Hotel sat in the middle of the village and in the middle of the sport's whole history, a square, plain, honorable house with the climbers' names in its registers going back before the railway, before the roads, before the fame. Whymper had left from its door in 1865. That was in the guidebooks. What the guidebooks did not say was who now stood in that door, which was the manager, Herr Imboden, a neat gray man with the eyes of a good bank teller, holding my letter of introduction and visibly performing the arithmetic of me.

I have stood in a great many doorways while a man does that arithmetic. The sum comes out differently in different countries. In my own, the calculation ran on one column only. Herr Imboden's ran on several, of which I could identify the letter from the firm, the nine cases of equipment, the season's emptiness at my back, and the house's hundred years of taking in whatever strange weather the mountains sent down, and the total, arrived at with no visible carrying, was that I was Herr Joiner of Chicago, that my room was the third floor south, and that supper was at seven.

"You will wish to know," he said, walking me up the stair himself, "that it is the room of Herr Fell."

"The firm hoped his effects might be kept safe."

"They are in the room." He unlocked the door and stood aside with the key held out, and for a moment the bank teller's eyes let something through that was older and less regulated, the look of a man who has kept a lamp lit without being asked. "He was our guest eleven summers, Herr Joiner. The bill was rendered to Chicago because the firm requires bills. The room would have waited regardless. In this house we do not sell a man's chair while he is on the mountain."

"And if he is not on the mountain?"

Herr Imboden put the key in my hand.

"Then the chair waits longer," he said, and went down to see about supper.

6. The Effects of Herr Fell

A hotel room that has waited nine months for a man tells you more about him than his own house would. A house accumulates a life entire, and the important things sink to the bottom, under the sediment of every year's habits. A room holds one season of one campaign, and everything in it was chosen, carried up a mountain valley at mule rates, and set down where the hand would want it. It is the difference between a biography and a field kit, and I would rather read a man's field kit any day.

I went through Fell's room that first night with a lamp and my inventory book, because Stroud was owed an accounting and because I could not have slept in it otherwise, among all those unexplained shapes. I set down here what the room held, in the order the lamp found it.

Item. On the washstand shelf, a razor strop worn to velvet, and a cake of Marseille soap gone to stone.

Item. Along the south wall, four plate cases of the firm's pattern, empty, their straps coiled and buckled, each coil laid the same hand's-turn, so that the empties themselves were stored like work.

Item. In the wardrobe, a winter coat too heavy for the season he had vanished in, which told me he had meant to come back before October; a formal collar, never worn, still in its paper; and on the floor beneath, a wooden box with rope handles, stenciled by his own brush: SPOILED PLATES — HOLD FOR DISCARD.

Item. On the table by the window, which he had plainly used for a desk, a spirit lamp, a loupe, a bottle of India ink dried to a scab, and a wooden frame the size of a prayer book, shut with a brass hook, which when opened proved to be a folding stereoscope of a private pattern, no maker's stamp, and in the rack of it a single card. The card was a portrait, made indoors by window light, of a woman of perhaps fifty, seated, in a dark dress, her hands quiet in her lap, looking at the camera the way people look only at a camera operated by someone they have loved a long time, with the face at ease and one eyebrow very slightly raised, holding the last word of a conversation the exposure had interrupted. In relief she sat in the little walnut theater of the viewer at a distance of about six feet, which is the distance of the other chair. I looked longer than my inventory required. Then I hooked the frame shut and did not open it again, and entered it in the book as Item, one stereographic portrait, private property of A. Fell, and I record as a matter of professional conscience that it is the finest photograph I have ever seen, and that no one will ever buy it, and that both of those facts are permanent, and were intended.

Item. One shelf of books. A German grammar, broken-backed. Tables of the Swiss survey. Ruskin, unread by the look of the spine, likely a gift. And Whymper, Scrambles Amongst the Alps, read nearly to pieces, rebound once in canvas by an amateur hand I judged to be Fell's own.

I sat down with the Whymper, because my own copy had eight days of the Atlantic in it and I wanted to see what eleven summers in this valley did to the same book. The answer was marginalia, in pencil, in the narrow upright hand. Most of it was a workman checking another workman: route times queried, a sneer at an engraving's shadows, light impossible for the hour claimed. But the chapter on the accident, the chapter I had read twice in a deck chair with my hat off, was the cleanest in the book. No marks at all, for eleven pages. Then, against the sentence recording that the body of Lord Francis Douglas was never found, one line, ruled with a straightedge, and in the margin a date and nothing else.

14 July 65 — 9 August 96.

I had copied Fell's last manifest into my notebook in Chicago. I did not need to open it to know the date on which he had exposed eleven plates of the Matterhorn glacier and listed them under one subject. I sat for a while with the lamp hissing gently and the shutters open, and the village went to bed under my window, and above the roofline, where the sky kept a paler blue than the street, the mountain stood up black with the last light on the very tip of it, a snuffed wick holding its spark.

Thirty-one years, less five weeks. A man does not rule a line like that with a straightedge and mean nothing arithmetical by it.

I will be honest about what I did next, because this account is worth nothing if I am not. I did not sit up all night with wild surmise. I closed the shutters, wrote the day's inventory fair, wrote to my mother, weatherlessly, and went to bed in Ambrose Fell's bed, and slept well, and in the morning I went to work on the firm's fifty-nine numbers, and I kept going to work on them for most of a month. I had glass to expose while the light lasted and a living to earn, and I had also a species of stage fright about that box marked SPOILED, which I did not permit myself to examine, the label being either true, in which case the box was nothing, or false, in which case it had been left as a lie in a locked room, addressed to whoever came after, and I was not yet ready to know which. A craftsman learns to let a glue-up cure. I am aware this reads like patience and was in fact its opposite, deferred.

The work of June wants a word, because the reader has by now heard the romance of the trade and is owed the freight bill.

To re-make a Fell composition, the procedure was this. Read the station note. Walk to the station, which the note locates by boundary stones, chapel corners, and boulders that have not moved since the ice let go of them, carrying seventy pounds of camera, glass, and tripod, generally uphill, generally before dawn. Find the exact prospect, which is not a place so much as a height and an angle, fifty-two inches mattering as much as fifty-two paces. Wait for the note's weather. Expose. Carry everything down. Develop by night in the cellar Herr Imboden had cleared for me among the wine, with the smell of fixer and Fendant contending, and compare the wet plate against the 1878 print, and learn, in the comparing, that the master had waited twenty minutes longer than you did, and that his twenty minutes were the picture.

For the carrying I had Clemenz Biner, nineteen, porter, of the guild of Zermatt porters who are all waiting to be guides as clerks wait to be partners. Herr Imboden had assigned him to me with the remark that the boy's English was the best in the village and his opinion of it better still. Clemenz's English had been acquired over four winters as boots at a Territet hotel and it was a glorious instrument, fluent, headlong, and mined throughout with hotel vocabulary deployed at altitude. He would top a moraine crest at four in the morning, set down seventy pounds of my livelihood, survey a five-mile prospect of glacier and precipice going gold at the edges, and pronounce it a very superior aspect, with every modern comfort, and I never once heard the phrase without my morning improving, which is a thing worth carrying to five thousand feet, whatever the manifest says.

It was Clemenz who took me, the second Sunday, after Mass, to the churchyard.

He did not make an excursion of it. The village does not. The graves are inside the ordinary business of the place, between the church door and the river, and the guides walk past them to work. He showed me where Michel Croz lies under his broken column with the medal of his profession carved in the stone, and the row where the English dead of various years are gathered as if for a group portrait nobody wanted taken, HADOW, and HUDSON close by within the English church, and the newer stones of seasons since, each with its altitude of grief, and then he stood with his cap in his hands and let me read.

"My grandfather help carry them down," he said at last. "From the glacier. Before my father is born, this. Three only, they find." He put his cap back on, which in Clemenz was the signal that a subject was closed and being shown the door. "The fourth gentleman, the young lord. Him the mountain keeps."

"Still?"

"Still." He looked up past the church roof, to where the mountain stood over the village in its afternoon smoke of cloud, with the patience of the very large, and then he said the thing that everyone in that valley knew and no one in my trade had thought to put on a shot list. He said it kindly, and with a shrug, the shrug of a man whose family has lived under a fact for thirty years as other families live under a mortgage.

"The ice is a river, Herr Joiner. Slow, but it runs downhill all the same. My grandfather says: what the mountain takes at the top, the glacier one day pays out at the bottom. It keeps its own books, that one." He picked up his rucksack, done with churchyards. "Only nobody stays for the paying. It is thirty years, forty. Who waits forty years at a bank?"

We went up to the Riffelberg after that and made Number 63 in weather Fell's note called for and the sky supplied, and it was, Clemenz declared, a first-class article. I worked until the light died. In the cellar that night, with the day's plates in the racks and the village asleep over my head, I stood a long while looking at a box I had not brought down with me, because it was still up in the room, holding whatever it held.

Who waits forty years at a bank?

I washed my hands in the dark, and went up, and took the box down from the wardrobe floor, and unbuckled the first strap. Then the church bell, forty feet from my shutters, rang eleven, and I mean the reader to know that I am reporting and not arranging things when I say that I buckled the strap again, and sat down instead, and wrote to Chicago for the Friday wire that all proceeded satisfactorily. Some doors want daylight. This is a superstition, and I hold it.

7. Boulders, Numbered

The Zmutt valley runs west out of Zermatt along the mountain's northern foot, and it was the part of the country I came to first love and last understand, which is a sequence I recommend. An hour's walk up from the village the path goes through the hamlet of Zmutt itself, a dozen larch houses black with three centuries of sun, standing on their staddle stones with their little barns among them, the whole settlement the size of a thought, and beyond it the valley opens and goes gray: moraine, milk-white river, and then the ice.

Nobody had told me about the sound. The Matterhorn glacier comes down off the north face in a broken apron, and the north face works on it all day, and four or five times in an afternoon there would come a crack like a quarry shot and then a long push of thunder while some cornice or serac went down the wall in a smoke of snow, and the first time it happened I had my head under the focusing cloth, and I record that Number 90 of the new series was very nearly a study of the photographer's own boot.

The shot list gave me four numbers in that valley. I took Clemenz and a tent, meaning to work it properly over three days, and it was on the first morning, on the crest of the old lateral moraine with the tripod half spread, that a voice behind me said, with an edge on it you could have shaved with:

"You are standing on my datum."

I looked down. Between my boots was a patch of white paint the size of a saucer, with a number on it: 22.

"I beg your pardon," I said, and moved, and the owner of the voice came past me without further examination, knelt at the paint, and set the heel of a surveyor's staff on it as though returning a borrowed tool to its peg. She was somewhere in her thirties, in gray tweed cut for work, with a canvas satchel gone white at the seams from rock, and she wore no hat against all local custom, her dark hair pinned up with the efficiency of a woman who has renegotiated her treaty with the wind and won. A hundred yards off, at a small theodolite on a tripod better than mine, a village boy stood waiting with the patience of the paid.

She took her sight, called a string of figures to the boy in French, wrote in a notebook bound in oilcloth, and only then looked at me again, at the camera, at Clemenz under his mountain of cases, and performed the whole examination in about a second and a half.

"You'd be the new view man," she said. Scots, the words squared off at the corners like ashlar. "The hotel said Chicago had sent someone." Her eyes went over my equipment like a customs officer's, looking for the lie in the manifest. "You people are early this year. The picture-postal trade doesn't usually infest this side before July."

"The infestation is four numbers and three days, ma'am, and I'll keep off your paint."

"Ma'am," she repeated, as though tasting a foreign coin. "Miss Forsyth will do. And it isn't the paint that concerns me, it's the practice. Your predecessors' idea of an improving view was to pose a client on stake fourteen with an ice axe he couldn't spell, kick the stake straight after, and cost me a season's figures. One of them," she said, with the level tone of a woman reporting an atrocity to a tribunal, "moved a boulder."

Clemenz, behind me, made a small noise of a man storing the story for the village.

"Moved it where?"

"Into his composition." She let that sit. "Nine hundredweight if it was a stone. He had four porters and no shame. The boulder was numbered, Mr. —"

"Joiner."

"— Mr. Joiner, it was numbered thirty-one, it had been numbered thirty-one for eleven years, and its whole use on this earth was that nobody moved it but the ice." She closed her notebook with a snap. "So you'll forgive the reception. What are your four numbers, and where do they stand?"

I told her: the mountain and glacier from the moraine crest, the hamlet, the ice grotto at the snout if the season had made one, and the Staffelalp prospect with the north face entire. She heard the stations the way a landlady hears references, and evidently they were respectable, because the edge came off, one full degree.

"The crest station's yours till noon, the light goes flat after. Keep to the crest line walking out; the inner slope is undercut this month and it goes without asking." She turned to leave, then paid me the compliment of a professional afterthought, thrown over the shoulder. "And if your glass is any good, take the ice at the snout while you're down there. It's doing something this summer it has not done in thirty years, and nobody in Europe is photographing it, which is the usual arrangement between events and photographers."

She was fifty yards gone before I understood I had been given a professional courtesy, however sharpened at both ends, and it wanted returning. I am my mother's son; I called after her.

"What did the boulder cost you?"

She stopped. Came back the fifty yards, which I later learned was the most Alexandra Forsyth ever conceded to anybody's curiosity, and looked at me properly for the first time, as a person rather than a trade.

"Eleven years of one line of figures," she said. "Which is eleven years of one man's evenings. My father plotted that stone through four hundred readings. The curve of its travel was the cleanest we had, and a fool in a boater bent it in an afternoon, and every paper we published after has an asterisk on it. That is what it cost. You're the first of your profession to ask." She considered me a moment longer. "The ice moves, Mr. Joiner. That surprises nobody; the peasants have known it for centuries, the physicists for sixty years. What my father gave his life to was the rate, the rate to the inch, season against season, year against year. To your trade this valley is scenery. It is in fact a machine, running slow, and we are measuring it."

"How fast does it run? Here, at the middle of the tongue."

"This month, about nine inches a day. High summer, a foot and more." She said it the way other women of her nation say the catechism. "Everything that stands on this ice, everything that has fallen into it, everything the mountain has fed it since before your republic and mine had names, is on its way down this valley at that rate. It is the slowest freight service in Europe, and it loses nothing. It only delivers late."

The boy at the theodolite called something then, weather coming over the Col d'Hérens, and she was Miss Forsyth of the survey again inside a second, notebook open, courtesy concluded.

"Miss Forsyth," I said, because there is a species of question that walks out of a man before his good sense can invoice it. "Did you know my predecessor? Fell, the man was called. He worked this valley eleven summers, and the last anyone heard of him, last August, he was photographing your glacier. The snout, from this moraine. Eleven plates of it."

I have spent my working life watching faces arrange themselves, it is half the craft of portraiture, and I watched hers do it now: watched something come up fast behind her eyes, look out at me, and be replaced by ordinary weather so smoothly that a man without my training would have sworn nothing had crossed at all. She squared her notebook against her satchel, in the manner of a person squaring more than paper.

"Everyone in the valley knew Mr. Fell by sight," she said. "Good day, Mr. Joiner. Mind the crest going out."

She went off up her line of numbered stones, calling figures, and the boy swung the little brass instrument after her like a compass needle finding its north again, and Clemenz and I stood alone on eleven thousand years of piled rubble with the mountain going smoky overhead.

Clemenz shouldered the cases. He had followed the entire exchange with the frank enjoyment of a village that has no theater and needs none.

"The Fräulein Doktor," he said, with reverence. "Two summers she is here. She pays cash, she falls never, and she is rude to everybody, in alphabetic order." He settled the straps, nodded up the moraine after her. "But you, Herr Joiner, she is rude to specially. This is a very good sign."

"Of what?"

"This I do not know," said Clemenz radiantly. "With ladies of quality I know only the signs, never the weather."

We made the crest number before the light flattened, and the hamlet in the afternoon, and that evening from the tent door I watched the survey work its way home down the far moraine, two small figures against a mile of gray, stopping at every patch of paint, and I thought about a curve bent in an afternoon, and about evenings, four hundred of them, one man's, and I confess the trade I thought hardest about that night was not my own. We had a vault of eleven hundred cabinets in Chicago and called it the visible world. This woman's father had spent eleven years on one stone, to learn one true number, and there was no vault anywhere that sold it.

The last thing I did by lantern light was open my notebook to the page where I had copied Fell's margin, the ruled line, the two dates thirty-one years apart, and under it I now wrote the day's acquisitions in a column, as the firm had trained me.

Ice at the tongue: 9 in./day, rising. It loses nothing. It only delivers late. She knew him. She said she knew him by sight.

Then, because the firm had trained me, I drew the line under the column where the sum goes, and left the sum blank, and blew out the light.

8. The Colonel of Relics

July opened the season the way a curtain opens a play. Inside of ten days the empty street became a promenade, the hotels put on their linen and their orchestras, sixty mules a day came up from the railhead under trunks with steamship labels, and the guides stood outside the Monte Rosa each morning in their black coats, being engaged, like cabs with summits. English came up in flocks, as foretold. So did Americans, French, Prussians, a Russian prince with his own cook, and one American the description of whom I have been postponing, I notice, for as long as the story will let me, in the manner of a man postponing a smell.

I met him first as a voice, on the Monte Rosa veranda, the second evening of July. The voice was doing business at the far rail, in tones of molasses poured warm, and what it was saying was this:

"Madam, I would not sell it to you. Not to you. To a tourist, yes, tomorrow, and may his conscience fit him better than mine would fit me. But you, madam, a woman of feeling, who has stood where we stood today and felt what this valley holds in its stone heart, to you I could only ever entrust it."

The object being entrusted, into the gloved hands of a wide-eyed lady from, I later learned, Cleveland, was a shallow velvet case, and in the case, on white satin, lay three inches of old rope.

The man entrusting it wore a suit of pale gray, a planter's hat, and about forty extra years of the American South applied like varnish. He was sixty, built generous, barbered to a silver point, and he moved through the veranda crowd with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been punched and cannot imagine the arrangements. His card, which I would come to possess several of, against my will, like burrs, read:

COL. BYRON K. PREWITT MEMPHIS · LUCERNE · THE WORLD Alpine Memorials & Historical Requisites

The rope wants explaining, and the explanation is the man entire.

Every guest in Zermatt knew the story of 1865 before their trunks were unstrapped; it was the local wine, poured with everything. And the actual rope, the very manila that had parted on the north face with four men below it, existed. A length of it lay in a glass case in the Monte Rosa's little parlor cabinet, brown as a root, next to a barometer and a chamois horn, and guests filed past it after dinner and were quiet. There was no charge to look. The Seilers would as soon have charged admission to a grave.

Colonel Prewitt's contribution to the memorial economy was to sell the rope retail.

Fragments, madam. Recovered, madam, in circumstances he was not at liberty to fully disclose, from the effects of a guide's estate, authenticated by experts whose names were themselves entrusted only to purchasers. Each fragment in velvet, each with its certificate, ivory laid paper, a seal like a bishop's. Clemenz told me the guides' joke, which was that the Colonel had sold, by the village's arithmetic, some four hundred feet of a rope that had measured two hundred, and that if the sales continued the rope would soon be long enough to have saved everybody.

The joke stopped there, because the rest was not funny. The rest was the photographs.

I found them on the third of July, in the window of a shop by the church bridge that let its sill to the Colonel for the season: a dozen cabinet cards, Memorial Portraiture by Appointment, and I stood at that window perhaps ten minutes, and a passerby would have taken me for a man admiring. What I was doing was reading a fraud the way a locksmith reads a scratch on a lock.

Understand the goods. A widow, or a mother, or a fiancée, twenty or thirty years along in her grief, sits in a rented parlor in her best black. The Colonel's operator makes her portrait. When the print comes home to her hotel, there is a second figure in it, behind her chair, faint as breath on glass, a young man with an ice axe, his face just short of legible, his hand on, or nearly on, her shoulder. And under the mount, in copperplate: He is nearer the summit now.

Double exposure. The oldest trick in a trade that was born owning it, older than I was, exposed twice on one plate, the ghost figure a confederate posed against black velvet, underexposed to a whisper. Any operator alive could make one in an afternoon. That was not what held me at the window. What held me was the workmanship, which was terrible, and the clientele, which was not. The ghost's boots, in two of the cards, were the same boots at the same angle, because the Colonel's man was too idle to repose his confederate between victims. The ghost's face in a third had been scratched on the negative to blur it, the scratches plain as plow marks under a loupe. It was cheap work, hasty work, work that despised its buyer, and its buyers were women who had crossed an ocean to be nearer a grave that did not exist, because the ice had never given one up, and who would take that card home to Surrey or to Boston and put it on a table by the bed and believe, every remaining evening of their lives, that the mountain had let him lean down once and touch her.

I have thought a long time about why that window put such a coldness through me, when my own firm sold sentiment by the carton and I had posed my share of it. I believe it is this. A stereograph of the Rigi sunrise says: here is a true thing you cannot come and see; hold it to the light and it will stand up honestly before you. The Colonel's card said: here is the one thing you want in the world, and it is a lie, and the lie is priced to what you have. Both trades run on longing. One of them feeds it, the other farms it.

He introduced himself to me, of course. Men like Prewitt inventory a village inside a week, and I was the item that didn't match the shelf: an American operator, colored, alone, with a mule-load of first-class equipment and the Stroud name behind him. I was developing at the cellar door when the pale suit arrived in it, hat already off, hand already out, warmth already at the full, like a lamp carried into a smokehouse.

"Colonel Byron Prewitt. And you are the Stroud man, and I am honored, sir, honored beyond the ordinary run of the word." I will spare the reader most of the aria. It ran eight or ten minutes and touched upon the dignity of the photographic art, the vulgarity of the age, his admiration, expressed with a connoisseur's particularity, for the firm's Egyptian series, and his certainty, delivered with a bow of exactly calibrated depth, that a man of my evident cultivation had experienced, in our shared republic, treatment unworthy of him, a subject on which the Colonel wished me to know his own views were fifty years in advance of the common. This last was the piece I admired. A crude man flatters your work. Prewitt had sized me at a glance and flattered my wounds.

"And your predecessor, now," he said, at the door, hat resuming, timing rehearsed to read as afterthought. "Fell. There was an artist of the genuine stamp. We had dealings, small dealings, seasons past. Whatever has become of him? One hears he left effects."

"One hears more than I do, Colonel."

"No doubt, no doubt." He smiled with everything but the eyes, which stayed on me, taking their own kind of exposure. "Well. A great house looks after its property. Should you ever find the firm's interests and mine in, let us say, adjacency, my card."

He entrusted me with the card and went up the cellar stair, and paused at the top of it, because men like Prewitt exit on a line, like actors.

"This valley, Mr. Joiner," he said, gazing out at the street with real, unfeigned satisfaction, the satisfaction of a farmer surveying bottomland, "is the finest property in the memorial line on this continent. Thirty years of mourning, sir, with a railway to it."

That same week I saw the two ropes pass each other, the sold one and the true.

It was outside the guides' room, morning, the street full of departures. Prewitt had a client at the shop sill, a fresh widow of the ordinary kind, not a mountaineer's, just death's, being shown the velvet cases, and ten feet away, going to work with a coil over his shoulder and an Englishman's rucksack in his hand, passed a thickset man of about fifty-five with a beard going gray and a face that had been outdoors since the Flood, and Clemenz beside me took off his cap, not to be seen doing it, and put it back.

"Taugwalder," he said, when the man was past. "Peter. The son. He was on it."

"On the mountain? In sixty-five?"

"Twenty-two years old. On the rope, Herr Joiner. The one that held." Clemenz watched the broad back go up the street. "His father also. Old Peter. Him the English talked about after." He looked at his boots, and gave me two sentences of it, no more, in the voice of a man handing over something the village keeps wrapped. "They said the old man cut it. It is a lie, the inquiry said also it is a lie, but a lie with legs, this one. It walked him to America and back. He is dead now nine years."

Down the street, the Colonel's voice rose warm on the morning air. To you, madam, I could only ever entrust it.

Peter Taugwalder the younger went by the shop window without turning his head by one degree, which is its own kind of looking, and shouldered through the hotel door to collect his Englishman, and I stood in the July sunshine between the two of them, the man who carried the rope and the man who sold it, and that evening, for the first time since I had buckled it shut in June, I took Fell's box marked SPOILED down from the wardrobe, and set it on the table, and lit both lamps.

9. Printed in the Cellar

The box held glass, as I had known since June it would, the weight and the small bright chime of it under the lid being past disguising. Eleven negatives, five-by-eight, the stereo pairs side by side on each plate, every one sleeved in Fell's brown paper, and on the top sleeve, in the narrow upright hand, an inscription that I read standing, and then read again sitting down.

Spoiled: all eleven. Exposure correct, development correct, subject correct. Spoiled by being true too slowly. If the firm's man reads German, the word is zu früh.

I did not read German, then. Clemenz rendered it for me later without being told where it came from: too soon. The same two words the loupe had found scratched on Number 87 in the vault in Chicago, nineteen years earlier, against a mountain four miles distant and a guide the size of a beetle. A man may carry a phrase his whole working life, as my father carried measure it standing, and I had now met this one at both ends of Ambrose Fell's, and I still did not know what it measured.

The plates themselves I took down to the cellar that same night to print, which the reader will have expected. What the reader may not expect, unless the reader has stood at a sink at one in the morning with another man's negatives, is the intimacy of the thing. Printing a stranger's plate, you stand where he stood in the most exact sense the world allows: his sky comes up in your dish, at the pace he metered for it, and every choice he made argues with every choice you would have made, in the red dark, at conversational range. By the third plate I knew Fell as I knew perhaps four living men. By the eleventh I could hear him.

Here is what the eleven said, laid out wet on the racks with the wine bottles looking on.

They were one subject, as the manifest had confessed: the snout of the Matterhorn glacier from the Zmutt moraine, the dead gray end of the ice where it breaks into rubble and the milk river runs out of a low arched cave in the ice wall, blue-shadowed, mean, and by every commercial standard of my firm and every other, nothing. No peak. No scale figure. No romance. Ice in the middle distance, dirt in the near, a subject Stroud had rated one number and would have cut from the list if cardboard cost a cent more.

Eleven times. Dated on the emulsion edge, the scratches tiny and orderly: 19 July, 24 July, 29 July, and so on at intervals of four or five days to 9 August. All in fair weather. All at the same hour, within minutes, the shadows agreeing like witnesses rehearsed.

And all, so far as the loupe could find, from the same station to the breadth of a tripod screw. Understand what that means in the field. To re-set a camera on rubble to the same inch, eleven times over three weeks, is not persistence, it is masonry. He had built a station and hidden the building: I would find, when I went up, three flat stones set in the moraine crest like pads for a transit, under the gravel, patient as Rome. The compositions were not similar. They were identical, in the way no two exposures of a living scene are ever identical, except that they were not identical, and it took me until nearly three in the morning, and the last two prints, to put my eye on the difference, because the difference was not in any thing. Every boulder held its place, plate over plate. The difference was in the ice, and it was small, and it was everywhere: the crevasse patterns walked, print to print, the way a sleeping dog's flank walks with its breathing. The glacier had sat for its portrait eleven times, and, like any living sitter across three weeks, it had not held still.

I want to say plainly that up to this point I had followed Fell's procedure without following his mind. I stood at the racks looking at eleven gray near-twins and thought, God forgive the trade that trained me, there is no card here.

Then, because my hands were idle while the last prints washed, and because his folding viewer was upstairs and I had brought it down, having told myself the instrument wanted testing after nine idle months, meaning that I wanted an excuse to be in its company: I set Marianne's portrait carefully aside, face down, as you turn a portrait when the room is about to hold something not hers; and I slid two of the wet prints into the rack together. Not a proper pair. The pair off one plate would have shown me the scene as any tourist's stereograph shows it, true depth, dull ice. What I racked, out of a printer's idleness at three in the morning, was the left half of the 19th of July beside the left half of the 9th of August. Same lens. Same station. Twenty-one days apart.

I put my eyes to the hoods, and the two prints poured together, and I am now going to attempt the thing I have failed to say aloud for many years, which is what happened in that instrument.

The mountain fused. The moraine fused, boulder onto boulder, and lay down flat as a painted drop, because to a stereoscope whatever has not moved between the two views is at infinity: no difference, no depth. All the dead country fused and flattened and retired.

The glacier refused. Every point of the ice had traveled those twenty-one days, and the instrument, which knows no time, only difference, read all that travel as parallax, and hung the whole tongue of the glacier out in front of the flattened world in a relief no natural view could ever own. I tell you the ice stood up off the plate. It came forward into the little walnut dark of the viewer like a thing shown against black velvet, every crevasse in relief, the whole dying snout of it presented to the eye as a jeweler presents a stone on cloth, and what the presentation said, said not in figures as the Fräulein Doktor's stakes said it but to the animal back of the eye, in the oldest language depth owns, was: this is not scenery. This is motion. The scenery is behind it; this thing is in transit.

Two views of one summer, and between them the truth that neither held alone. My trade had spent fifty years putting two eyes a nose apart to make space stand up in a parlor. Fell had set his two eyes three weeks apart, and made time stand up. There is no other man in the story of the craft, I believe, who would have thought of it, and I include myself with no discount, because I did not think of it either. I only inherited it, at three in the morning, wet to both elbows, with my heart going like a watch in a fist.

That was the first thing. The second thing was small, as the large things in this account will keep insisting on being.

Deep in the print, at the snout, where the melt river came out of its arched cave and the ice wall stood in its own blue shadow, there was a point of light. In any single print I would have passed it for a wet fleck on the paper, or a glint of quartz in the rubble, the snout of a glacier being a scrapyard of the mountain's mineral opinions. In the fused view it could not be passed, because it did not lie with the rubble plane and it did not lie at infinity with the honest rock. It hung in the ice, at the ice's own uncanny forward relief, a single bright grain in the shadowed wall above the cave mouth, and when I changed prints, July 24th against August 4th, any left half against any later left half, my hands by now not entirely reliable, it was in every fusion, always in the wall, always with the ice.

Whatever threw that glint was inside the glacier, near enough its skin to catch morning sun through melt, and it was riding the ice down the valley at the freight rate Miss Forsyth had given me on the moraine with her back straight and her voice level: nine inches a day, rising. Metal, said the printer in me, or glass, or the dressed nailheads of a boot sole, and the rest of me stood very still and said nothing at all, because over the racks, in the red light, I had remembered the sleeve. Exposure correct, development correct, subject correct. Spoiled by being true too slowly.

He had not been photographing ice.

He had been photographing an arrival. Eleven times, at four-day intervals, a man of fifty-six had walked two hours up a moraine to stand at a masonry station and document the progress of the slowest freight in Europe, and in August he had ruled a line in a book against a sentence about a nineteen-year-old boy the mountain never gave back, and had done the arithmetic thirty-one years of ice had been doing all along, and had understood, zu früh, too soon, that the delivery would not come that season, and had shut the box, and lettered a lie on the lid to keep the firm's hands off the truth, and had gone away somewhere to wait out the winter with the patience of a man who has, at last, after a whole life in the trade of other people's monuments, found the one photograph that could not be taken by standing where anyone had stood before.

The wash water ran on over the prints with the sound small water makes in a stone room. I sat on a wine crate until the red lamp paled and the gray of morning came down the cellar stair, doing sums I had no tables for and getting, over and over, the same remainder, which was a question: how far up the ice, and how long?

Clemenz found me there at six, come to load for the day's number on the shot list, a waterfall above Täsch, very superior, every modern comfort. He looked at the racks, and at me, and at the racks.

"Change of program?" said Clemenz.

"Change of program," I said. "Zmutt."

10. Arithmetic

Fell's station was where the prints said it would be, on the moraine crest two hundred paces down-valley from paint number 22, and Clemenz found the stones inside a quarter hour, three flats set level under two inches of gravel, with a chisel mark on the center stone for the tripod's leading foot. He knelt over them a long time, reading them the way his people read snow.

"Built to stay the winter," he said. "He means to come back, Herr Joiner. A man does not lay foundation for one season." He stood and dusted his knees and looked down the gray mile of rubble to the snout, where the river ran out of its blue arch, and I watched him do the sum I had been doing since three in the morning. Clemenz had carried for surveyors before he carried for me. He knew what a fixed station was for. "Or," he said slowly, "he means for the picture to come to him."

"What do you know about pictures coming to people?"

"I know the Fräulein Doktor asks every week the same question of the same stones," said Clemenz, "and the ice answers a little different every time, and one summer, my grandfather says, the answer arrives at the bottom." He shouldered the tripod. "You will want to talk to her."

"I will want to talk to her."

"She is at stake nine since seven o'clock," said Clemenz, in the tone of a concierge who has already sent up the card.

⁂

She heard me out on the open moraine with the wind pushing at both of us, and I gave it to her straight and in order, the box, the sleeve, the eleven dates, the margin ruled in the Whymper, because a woman who numbers boulders has earned a report in her own language, and when I finished she said nothing for the length of three gusts. Then she held out her hand for the folding viewer.

I had racked the pair on the walk up, July 19th against August 9th. She stood with her boots set apart, steadied the instrument against the wind as though it were her own theodolite, and looked, and I watched Alexandra Forsyth meet the thing I had met in the cellar. Her shoulders did not move. Her breathing did. When she lowered the viewer at last her eyes went straight down the moraine to the snout, to the arch, refocusing from the little walnut theater to the actual gray world that theater had just informed on, and her face had the carefulness of a person carrying a full vessel over uneven ground.

"How does it do that," she said. Not a question, an audit. I explained the principle in four sentences, the two views set apart in time instead of space, the instrument reading change as depth, whatever rides the ice standing forward off the dead rock. She nodded once at the theory, an examiner's nod at a proof she would re-derive herself that evening, and handed back the viewer, and said, "My father would have given ten years of his life for that instrument, and he had no ten to give. Come to the Riffelalp at four. Ask for my sitting room. We are past discussing this on open ground."

She was forty paces gone up her line before she turned and added, with the wind taking half of it, "And Mr. Joiner. Thank you for the report. It was properly made," which I record because from that quarter, that season, it was the Legion of Honor.

⁂

The Riffelalp hotel stood on its shelf above the valley, and her sitting room was the cheapest it had, north-facing, one window, no view a tourist would pay for, and every flat surface in it under paper: rolls of profile drawings, tables in a dead man's hand annotated in a living one, and on the wall, where another woman of her class would have hung a watercolor, a surveyed longitudinal section of the Matterhorn glacier, inked like an anatomy.

She poured tea without asking and put the milk in first, decisively, Scots fashion, and began in the middle, having evidently decided during the intervening hours where the middle was.

"My father read the letter on the fourteenth of July, 1866. One year to the day, which the newspapers enjoyed. You know the substance of the accident; the world does. What the world has forgotten is what could not be found. Three men were recovered from the glacier within the week and lie in Zermatt. The fourth, Lord Francis Douglas, nineteen years of age, was not recovered. A boot, a belt, a pair of gloves. Nothing else, then or since. The Alpine men said the rocks of the north face kept him. My father read the accounts, looked at his own work, and wrote to the Journal that the rocks keep nothing on that face; that the face feeds the glacier; and that the glacier is a conveyance with a schedule; and that ice moving at the observed rates would carry a burden from the foot of that wall to the snout in something between twenty-eight and thirty-six years, and would then, in his phrase, render its account. He proposed that the survey be funded so the rate could be refined and the family spared a surprise." She set down the pot. "He signed it, which was his error. Professors may calculate anything. They may not calculate that, it seems, with a name attached."

"They took it badly."

"They took it as grave-robbery performed with a slide rule. One reviewer coined a phrase that followed him down every corridor in Edinburgh for twenty-seven years." She said it without inflection, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "The ghoulish arithmetic. He was a gentle man, Mr. Joiner. He measured ice because it was the largest patient thing in creation and he loved patient things. He answered the reviewers once, one letter, citing the Bossons precedent, and never again."

"Bossons?"

"Mont Blanc. 1820. Three guides taken by an avalanche into the ice; Forbes and others computed the flow; and in 1861, forty-one years on, at the foot of the Glacier des Bossons, the remains came out within a season of the estimate. It is the one experiment of its kind ever completed, and it proved the method entire." She looked at the inked anatomy on the wall. "Proved it for Chamonix. Chamonix rates, Chamonix ice. This glacier is steeper, colder, fed differently; my father's window here was his own, derived from his own line, and the Alpine Journal called it ghoulish, and the funding went elsewhere, and he measured on his own purse, eleven summers out of the next twenty-seven. He re-derived the window four times as the data thickened. It narrowed each time and its center never moved. I can show you the sheets. The center of my father's window, Mr. Joiner, is the season now outside that door."

The room was quiet except for the hotel being a hotel somewhere below us.

"He died in the winter of '93," she said. "In March, at the end, he had the notebooks brought to the bed, which the doctor permitted because the doctor was a fool of the kindest sort, and he put his finger on the last line of the last table and said to me: someone must be at the bottom when it proves. Not for the Journal. Because a thing this patient deserves a witness, and the boy deserves a churchyard." She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, her one gesture of the whole recital. "I lecture arithmetic at a school for girls. I have two months a year and the wrong sex for the Alpine Club, whose members have subscribed, over the years, eleven pounds to this survey, mostly to be rid of my letters. This is my third summer on my father's line. Now you know what I am doing on the moraine, which the village has wondered agreeably about for two years."

"And Fell?"

"Ah," she said. "Confession." She rose and stood at her north window. "Your Mr. Fell came to me on the moraine the last week of July, a year ago. I knew him for the view man; everyone did; the black coat and that mule. He was civil. He asked intelligent questions about the survey, which from a photographer disarmed me, and then he asked the one question, and the asking of it in a stranger's mouth frightened me, if you want the truth. He said: Madam. At the rates you publish, a burden taken in at the top in July of '65: when does the ice pay it out, and where do I stand? No preamble. Like a man at a booking office." She turned around. "I told him my figures were not for the picture trade. He looked at me the way a man looks who has been handed back his own sin in the wrong change, thanked me, and went down the moraine, and five weeks later the village said the view man had paid his bill and gone, and I confess I was relieved. I took him for a scavenger with a lens, Mr. Joiner. A quality scavenger. The kind that would sell the arrival."

"And now?"

"Now I have looked into that instrument of his," she said, "and I find he was doing my father's work with tools my father never had, and asking my question with his hat in his hand, and I sent him away, and he built a stone station and did it alone, and wherever he is at this hour he is the one man in Europe who believed my father without being related to him." She came back to the table and sat down with the squared decision of a chairman gaveling herself to order. "So. Terms."

We made them in twenty minutes, and they held, which is more than most treaties manage, and I set them down here as they were spoken.

Her figures and his plates would be put together, hers the rate, his the position, and the sum kept by the two of us and Clemenz and no other soul until it ripened. Nothing to my firm; a catalogue house had no claim on a churchyard. Nothing, on any account, at any price, under any form of words, to Colonel Byron K. Prewitt, whom she pronounced without heat as a fact of the season, like the horseflies. When the window's center approached, a letter would go privately to the family in Scotland, hers to write, she having the language of such letters and I not, offering them silence or presence as they should choose. If the ice delivered, the family's wishes governed everything thereafter: the law, the church, the newspapers told or never told. If it did not deliver, we would strike the tents in October, say nothing, and she would come back with her stakes the next year, and the next, for as long as schools have summers.

"And Fell?" I said, at the door.

"Fell has the only other set of these facts on earth and a stone station on my moraine," said Alexandra Forsyth. "Find him, Mr. Joiner, before the season does. A man who has waited thirty-one years for one photograph will not send out cards announcing where he stands. But he is standing somewhere on my line."

I went down the path to the village in the long gold evening with the folding viewer buttoned inside my coat, and did the day's last professional act at the Monte Rosa desk, which was to hand Herr Imboden the Friday wire for Chicago. ALL PROCEEDS SATISFACTORILY. Forty-one numbers remain. WEATHER FAVORABLE.

It was the first dispatch of my career that was false in every clause, including the weather, which broke that night on a south wind and rained for three days, and I lay listening to it on the roof of Ambrose Fell's room, doing the new arithmetic, which had a window, and a center, and the center was August.

11. The Weak Rope

The rain held Zermatt indoors for three days, and a resort indoors is a pressure vessel, and the Monte Rosa managed the pressure as it had managed everything for sixty years, with soup, whist, and rationed anecdote. On the second evening, when the parlor had exhausted the day's newspapers and a Manchester cotton man had twice proposed charades, Herr Imboden crossed to the little cabinet between the barometer and the chamois horn, unlocked it with a key from his own ring, and lifted out the glass case with the rope in it, and the room went quiet before he had turned around.

He did this, I came to learn, perhaps twice a season, by a private almanac of his own: when the weather was foul, when the company was decent, and when some regulating instinct told him the story was about to be told anyway, badly, by somebody else, and had better be told right.

I will give it as he gave it, shorn of my questions and the cotton man's, because Imboden's version was the valley's version, and the valley had carried it thirty-two years and knew where every handhold was.

⁂

In July of 1865, seven men left this door at half past five in the morning. Whymper, the Englishman, who had tried the mountain eight times from the Italian side; Lord Francis Douglas, nineteen, a soft-spoken boy who had done a new route on the Obergabelhorn the week before and whom the guides liked, which was rarer than the guidebooks make out; the Reverend Charles Hudson, the strongest amateur of his age; young Hadow, nineteen also, Hudson's friend, with one season's experience, which mattered after; Michel Croz of Chamonix, a guide other guides watched to learn; and the two Peters, Taugwalder the father, forty-five, of this village, and Taugwalder the son, twenty-two, of this village, who on fine mornings still takes his coffee at the third table there by my dining-room window.

They found the mountain easier than thirty years of legend had promised, that is the terrible hinge of it, and at a quarter before two the next afternoon all seven stood on the summit, and the Italians, coming up the far side, were still hundreds of feet down, and the English party rolled stones to let them know the mountain was taken. One hour they had up there. It is a good summit, gentlemen; I have never stood on it; the men who have say you can see this hotel.

On the descent, Croz went first, then Hadow, then Hudson, then Lord Douglas, then the elder Taugwalder, then Whymper, then the son. On the difficult shoulder, above the north face, Hadow's boot went out. He took Croz over; the rope snapped Hudson from his hold, then Douglas. Four men were on the face. The elder Taugwalder braced against rock with the rope about his body, and the rope came taut, and held, and then did not hold. It parted between Taugwalder and Douglas, and the three who remained watched the four who did not, sliding, then falling free, four thousand feet to the glacier, and I will not describe that further, and no one who was there ever would.

Three days later the men of this village went out at dawn, my own father among them, and brought back what the glacier surface offered: Croz, Hudson, Hadow. The English chaplain read the service. Of the young lord there was found a boot, a belt, a pair of gloves. His mother wrote to this village every year until she was too old, asking whether the mountain had given anything more. The mountain has not.

⁂

Here Imboden paused and permitted the parlor its silence, and then, because he was a hotelier and not a dramatist, he opened the case and laid the rope along the table runner under the lamp, and let the fact do what facts do.

I had seen it through the glass a dozen evenings. I had not seen it out, and out was different. It was perhaps four feet of it, three strands, manila, gone the brown of old harness, and it was thin. That is the whole testimony. You have seen stouter cord on a butcher's parcel. Every rope in the guides' room across the street that season, 1897, would have held a fallen horse; this thing had been asked to hold four falling men, and the frayed end of it, teased out like a paintbrush left in a jar, said what it had said on the mountain.

"There were on that climb," said Imboden, re-coiling it with the handling of a sexton, "two hundred feet of good English rope, the Alpine Club's own pattern, and more of a second quality, also sound. And there was this, an old line, brought for spare, for fixing to rocks and leaving behind. When the party roped for the descent, this is what stood between the old guide and the young lord. At the inquiry the question was asked. Why the weak rope, there, of all places on earth?"

The cotton man, who had been good as gold for a quarter hour, could not help himself. "And the answer?"

"The answer of the inquiry," said Imboden, "was that there being insufficient good rope for all intervals when the party joined into one column, the old line was used for the last interval, without design, in the ordinary confusion of a great day. The elder Taugwalder was examined and acquitted of every imputation. That is the answer of the inquiry, and this house has never permitted any other under its roof." He shut the case. His voice did not change at all, which was how you knew the next sentence was the load-bearing one. "The answer of the London drawing rooms was otherwise. It was whispered, then printed with the whisper's courage, in the form of a question, which is how such things are always printed: whether an old peasant, seeing what was coming to a party of gentlemen, might not have chosen for his own interval a rope that would... release him from their company. No court said it. No signature carried it. It required none. Peter Taugwalder the elder was the second man ever to stand on that mountain, and he finished his life having it explained to him, by silence in this street, in the room of the guides, at the tables of the hotels, that he was either a fool or a murderer, with the choice left civilly open."

"He went to America," I said.

"For some years. Yes. There is a kind of talk a village cannot make and cannot stop, Herr Joiner, and an ocean is the one answer to it. It did not answer. He came home, and guided small, and died on the path above Findelen nine years ago, and the men who carried him down were the sons of the men who had gone silent, and every one of them would have given a season's wages to have the silence back. A village knows what it did, gentlemen, long after it has lost the power to undo it. You will find us very attentive, in these matters, ever since. It is not virtue. It is the scar."

He locked the cabinet and announced, in the same breath, that the barometer was rising and the dining room would open at seven, because that is how a hotelier tells a parlor the audience is ended.

⁂

The barometer kept its word. Two mornings later I finally made Number 78 of the list, The Guides of Zermatt, nineteen black coats in front of the guides' room wall, a picture that wanted an hour of diplomacy for every minute of optics, the question of who stood nearest whom in the front rank involving precedence structures older than some cantons. Taugwalder I put at center by the simple protocol of asking him first and letting the rest arrange themselves about the fact. He stood the exposure like church.

Afterward, while Clemenz cased the camera and the coats dispersed to their engagements, Peter Taugwalder the younger did not disperse. He waited until the street had taken the others, examining my tripod head as if pricing it, and then said, in the deliberate English of a man who has carried gentlefolk for forty years:

"You do not ask me things. All summer. The other photographers, always, they ask me things."

"You've been asked enough, I judged."

He considered that, turning the tripod's brass wing nut one way and the other. "You go much to the Zmutt," he said. "You and the lady of the stakes." It was not a question, so I did not answer it, and my silence was itself an answer, and we both knew the shape of what we were not saying, there in the emptying street. Half the village, I later understood, had drawn its own conclusions about the survey years since, and had been keeping the lady's secret for her with the terrible discretion of the scarred.

"I will tell you one thing," said Taugwalder. "Not for your firm. For your notebook, if you keep an honest one." He set the tripod gently against the wall, and stood straight, and delivered it as men deliver testimony they have not been believed on for thirty years, without heat, the heat having been used up somewhere in the 1860s.

"On the shoulder, when we roped down, I stood beside my father. I saw his hands. He tied the young lord with that rope twice about the body, twice, with the knot doubled, because the lord was light, and my father would have an extra turn on a light man. You do not give a double turn of any rope to a man you mean to lose." He picked up his rucksack. "The inquiry wrote it down and the drawing rooms did not print it. Rooms print the question, never the hands. My father's hands were the truest thing on that mountain, Herr Joiner. I saw them. I am the last alive who saw them."

He nodded once, the interview concluded by the same authority that had convened it, and went up the street to his Englishman, and I stood by the guides' wall a long moment with my own hands still smelling of brass and settled one thing finally in my mind, which I carried to the Riffelalp that same evening and put on Alexandra Forsyth's table beside her father's tables.

If the ice at the bottom held the boy, the ice held the rope's other end, and the rope's other end held the knots, and the knots were the last honest witnesses of the fourteenth of July, 1865: the question the drawing rooms had printed, answerable at last, in three turns of thirty-year-old manila.

"Then we are agreed on the order of things," she said, when she had heard me out. "My father's arithmetic brings us to the spot. Your optics tell us the hour. But whatever the ice renders, Mr. Joiner, the first reading of it belongs to neither of us." She looked out her north window, toward the village far below, where the lamps were coming on. "It belongs to the third table by the dining-room window."

12. Over the Théodule

The parcel record was the postmaster's to give or withhold, and he withheld it for nine days, on principle, the principle being that a Swiss official is not an almanac to be consulted by every passing firm. What moved him in the end was not my letter of authority. It was Herr Imboden, who walked across the street with me on the tenth morning and said three sentences to him in dialect, of which I understood one word, Fell, and the postmaster looked at me over his spectacles for a while, and then opened his ledger of forwardings as though opening the parish register of souls.

One entry. November the 19th, 1896. A parcel of books, forwarded at the addressee's standing instruction to: A. Fell, presso Pensione Ghiglione, Vernazza, Liguria, Regno d'Italia.

"Liguria," said Clemenz, reading over my shoulder, in the tone of a man confirming a long-held suspicion about foreigners. "The sea." He gave it the pronunciation mountain people keep for the sea: a rumor's.

I wired Stroud that the Italian numbers on the shot list would be made that fortnight, which was true, and that I was pursuing the firm's merchandise, which was also true, and I have noticed all my life that the most useful truths come in pairs like that, each holding the other's coat. Three numbers of the old High Alps had been made from the south side of the mountain: the Matterhorn from Breuil, where it stands up as a different mountain entirely, a horn of Africa, Fell had written in his station notes, grown in Italy out of Italian rock and slandered by the Swiss profile; the Théodule pass itself; and the towers of the Valtournenche. The direct road to all three, and to Liguria beyond, went over the glacier at eleven thousand feet, and the road had been in use, Clemenz assured me, since the Romans, whom he cited as other men cite their grandfathers.

We crossed on the last Tuesday of July: myself, Clemenz, and a Valtournenche man named Fabien picked up at the Gandegg hut, because custom and sense both said the glacier wanted three on the rope. We left at two in the morning under a moon you could have read contracts by.

I had been two months photographing glaciers. This was my first morning standing on one, and the difference is the difference between the gallery and the cage. The Théodule at that hour was a plain of gray iron, tilted, endless, creaking. Underfoot it was not silent and it was not loud; it ticked, and now and then, somewhere under the crust, at a depth the mind declined to price, something shifted its shoulder and the whole plain said a word in a language older than water. The rope ran moon-white from my waist forward to Fabien and back to Clemenz, and I understood inside the first mile why the men of this trade speak of the rope the way sailors speak of the ship, as the actual party, of which the persons attached are members.

Near the top of the pass, in the blue hour, Fabien stopped us with one lifted hand, prodded the snow ahead with his axe, twice, gently, the way you wake a sleeper you respect, and a yard of the road in front of him sighed and went down without haste into a blackness that did not report a bottom. We stood, the three of us, breathing, and looked into it, and the cold that came up out of that slot was not the morning's cold. It was the building's cold, the cellar-breath of the whole structure, and I thought, with a clarity I have never managed since at sea level: this is what Forsyth's tables measure and my plates portray, this black patience, and both of us are courtiers describing a king from the anteroom, and here is the audience chamber door, open a yard.

Fabien found the road's better opinion twenty feet to the west, and we went on, and at half past six we stood on the frontier, a ridge of wind-raked rock with Italy falling away below in a hundred miles of blue and bronze, and Clemenz, who had made the crossing before, produced from his rucksack, with the timing of a great head waiter, three ends of bread and a tin of anchovies, the specialty of the house.

The Matterhorn from the Théodule at seven in the morning was Number 96 of the new series, and I made it with the wind trying my patience and the tripod's, and it is as good as anything I have signed my life to, and the firm sold it for forty years, and not one purchaser in ten thousand can have known that the operator's hands were still remembering, through the whole exposure, a yard of road going down without haste into the dark.

⁂

Breuil in 1897 was six buildings and a chapel at the top of a green wilderness, with the south face of the mountain standing over it like a proclamation. Old Élie Pession kept the inn, and mule traffic, and the passing history of the valley, and when he understood, over soup, whose trail I walked, he sent his granddaughter for a better bottle and settled in to render accounts.

Yes. The English signore of the photographs. Late in September, last year, over from Zermatt with two cases and the black mule he had hired eleven summers running, always the same mule, a beast of prejudices, and the signore its one exception. He had lodged four nights, paid in silver, photographed nothing, which Pession had found notable, a man carrying a church organ across the Alps and declining to play it. He had sat evenings on the bench there, by the door, looking up at the mountain.

"Ours is the true face of it," said Pession, through Fabien's French and my own, the granddaughter correcting both. "The Swiss have the profile, we have the face. The signore said to me: from Breuil the mountain looks like what it is. I asked him: and what is it? And he thought a long time, this man, a serious man, and he said: a creditor." Pession spread his hands. "Then in the morning he went down the valley with the carrier to Châtillon, for the railway, and the mule went home over the pass with my nephew, and that is all of it, except that in the spring, in May, he came back through, going up, with provisions for a long stay bought in Châtillon, and he did not lodge, and he did not sit, and when my granddaughter asked him would he take soup, he thanked her like a bishop and said he was expected." He refilled my glass. "Expected. I have thought about the word. There is nothing up that road, signore, but the pass, and after the pass, Zermatt. And no one in Zermatt, by your own account, has seen him."

"There is one thing up that road," said Fabien slowly, "besides the pass."

Pession looked at him, and something passed between the two Valtournenche men at the pace of local knowledge, and Fabien turned to me. "The high pastures on the Swiss side. Above the Zmutt. A man who leaves the mule road below the Gandegg and goes north, over the shoulder, comes down to the alps at Staffel and Schönbiel with no one in Zermatt the wiser. It is not a tourist's road. It is a smuggler's road, and a shepherd's."

"And a photographer's," I said, "if the photographer had a reason."

Nobody at the table asked what the reason might be, Pession because he was an innkeeper, Fabien because he was on wages, and Clemenz because he had been beside me on the moraine when Fell's three stones came out from under the gravel, and had the whole ledger added long since. He only looked at me across the lamp with his eyebrows at half staff, a man declining to say I have told you the ice keeps books in front of strangers.

⁂

We made the two remaining Italian numbers the next day, and the day after that came down the Valtournenche on the carrier's cart through a morning of hayfields and church bells, every village smaller and warmer, the mountain shutting itself away behind shoulder after green shoulder until it was gone entire, which no one had warned me a mountain could be, and by evening we were at Châtillon on the floor of the great valley of Aosta, in vineyards, in summer, in Italy, and the train came in from Turin smelling of coal and peaches.

I sent Clemenz home over the pass with Fabien and the equipment, all but one case, there being fifty-nine reasons of the firm's for the equipment not to holiday at the seaside, and Clemenz took his wages and his orders, the orders being to keep the catalogue's appearance of progress and his own counsel, and to learn, by the harmless means at which the village excelled, who was buying milk and cheese this summer at the alps above the Zmutt, and to have the answer by my return.

"And if it is him?" said Clemenz, at the carriage window.

"Then nothing. Say nothing, do nothing, and above all photograph nothing. Just know where he sleeps."

The train took me down the Aosta valley in the last of the light, and at Turin I changed for the coast in the dark, and slept sitting up through Alessandria and woke somewhere south of Genoa because the air through the ventilator had changed its whole doctrine, salt and pine tar and heat that did not leave with the sun, and when I put up the blind the Mediterranean was running along below the track in the moonlight, close enough to hit with a cap, patient as ice and quicker about it, and I sat with my forehead on the cold glass like a boy, sixty hours from a glacier, watching my third sea.

13. The Lemon Terraces

The railway serves the Cinque Terre grudgingly, in the manner of a thing built through a country rather than to it, diving from tunnel to tunnel with the five villages doled out between like cards off a deck, a second apiece: a fistful of colored houses jammed in a crease of cliff, a strip of bright water, then rock again and the dark. I got down at the third card, at seven in the morning, into a heat with weight to it, and walked out of the little station and down a street the width of my outstretched arms, and the street opened, and there was Vernazza.

I had been paid for eleven years to look at places with a view to selling them, and the habit runs deep and I do not apologize for it: my first thought, entire and professional, was the firm has nothing like this and would ruin it. The town is one street and a hundred stairways. It pours down its ravine to a small stone-armed harbor, the one true harbor on that whole iron coast, and stops at the water as though caught mid-stride. The houses stand shoulder to shoulder at the quay, five and six stories, too tall for their width like books packed on end, and they are painted the colors bakers use, crust and crumb and burnt sugar and that rose the Italians get by letting red age forty years in salt air. On the seaward rock a round watchtower. On the harbor's other horn, a church with an octagonal belfry, gray and plain, its foundations in the waves' business. Nets on the quay. Lemons on the terraces above, going up the cliff in stone-walled steps to where the vines take over, a thousand years of wall, the whole mountainside worked by hand into a ladder of gardens, and over all of it a light that did not behave like my light. Alpine light comes down like judgment. This came off the water as well as the sky, from below as well as above, so that shadows filled with reflected sea and nothing on that waterfront was ever wholly dark. I stood at the street's mouth with my one case, working out the optics of it, and a woman above me cranked open a shutter and hung out a mattress to air, and looked down at me with frank interest and no alarm at all, and called something inquiring, of which I understood one word, because the word was Ghiglione.

The village had sorted my business before I had. I was a foreigner with a photographer's case, and the village had a category waiting, stitched and worn the winter long: another one belonging to the signore.

⁂

The Pensione Ghiglione was four rooms above a boat store, kept by the widow Ghiglione, a small square woman of sixty with forearms a smith would respect and a black dress gone soft gray at the seams, and she received me on her stairs, heard the name Fell in my first sentence, and did the thing I had crossed the Alps and half a kingdom being braced for. She looked at me a long moment, and nodded as though a delivery long expected had finally rounded the point, and said, in the French of the coast, which is Italian wearing a borrowed hat: "You will want coffee. He said you would come in the summer. He said you would be tired, and behind time, and honest, and I was to feed you before any talking." She turned up the stairs, then paused and inspected me again over her shoulder, item by item, and delivered the signore's full prophecy with a widow's scruple for the record. "He did not say you would be so tall."

That is how I learned that Ambrose Fell had left not a trail but a sieve, and that I was the grain that had come through it. The postal forwarding, standing unrevoked in a Swiss ledger where only a man tracing plates would ever ask; the box in the wardrobe with its lie lettered on the lid, a lie exactly transparent to one kind of reader; the room's effects in their eloquent order. He had baited the road at every fork with things only an operator would stop for, and the operator had stopped, and had printed the rejects at one in the morning as intended, and had crossed the Théodule as intended, and now stood being fed bread and anchovies and hill wine at eight in the morning as intended, by a widow with instructions eleven months old.

She fed me. Then she talked, three winters' worth.

He had come in November, half dead of a cough he called a nuisance, with a trunk and a case of books, and had taken the north room, the cheap one, sea glimpse from one corner of the window if you stood like a heron, and the village had him classified within the week: il fotografo inglese, elderly, courteous, mad, out on the breakwater before dawn in December watching the light happen with his hands in his pockets and no camera at all, which confirmed the madness and, in a fishing town, commanded a certain respect. The rehabilitation from mad to beloved had gone by the usual staircase, which is competence. He had mended her pump. He had rehung the shutter whose hinge-scream the whole quay knew by name. He had gone out with the boats twice, been sick once, learned the dialect words for the six kinds of wind, and paid his bill on Fridays in silver with the rent of a man who had noticed the room was cheap and quietly doubled it.

And in February, with the north room's light at its cleanest, he had unpacked the case that was not books after all, and begun, at close to sixty years of age, in a room eleven feet square smelling of tar and lemons, what Signora Ghiglione called his true winter's work, and what I, who was shown it that afternoon hanging in the houses of a fishing village, will go to my grave calling the finest sustained photography of the age, and no one in my trade has ever heard of it, and the trade's ignorance is the exact measure of everything the trade is.

He had photographed the village. That says nothing. Every excursionist with a hand camera photographs the village. He had photographed the village the way the village would have photographed itself if walls had eyes and mothers ran the printing frame: the widow's daughter at her loom with the shuttle blurred to a moth because he chose the loom's own exposure over a posed stillness; the men mending the great seine on the quay, sixty feet of net between eight pairs of hands, an entire economy legible in one look; old Berto the caulker asleep against his own upturned boat at noon, laid out with the dignity of a crusader on a tomb; and in house after house, up and down the stairways of that town, in tin frames on chests of drawers, the portraits. He had made portraits all winter, of anyone who fed him, and given the prints as rent for the feeding, and they hung now where the saints hang, and they deserved the placement. The signora's own was in the kitchen: her husband, drowned off Palmaria in 1881, existing until last winter as one waxen daguerreotype made at his conscription. Fell had copied it, and then, by darkroom means I partly reconstructed and partly still marvel at, had taken the tin stiffness out of the boy's face and the death out of the shadows and printed him warm, on matte paper the color of church candles, so that the man looked out of the frame at his widow across a kitchen he had never stood in and seemed on the point of asking what was for supper.

"He said," reported Signora Ghiglione, laying supper, "that it was the only work he ever signed."

It was true. In the corner of every print in that village, small as a grain of rice, in pencil: A.F.

⁂

The trunk was in the boat store below, under an old sail, corded and sealed, and she showed it to me as one shows a grave, and told me its standing orders: to be held until All Saints; if he had not returned or written by then, to go, unopened, to the priest, with money for Masses. What was in it I never learned, then or after, and I have thought about it on and off for the rest of my life, the way you think about a book seen once in a foreign shop window after hours, and I set that down here plainly so the reader is not waiting for the lid to open. It does not open. It was his, and it stayed his, and there is a whole popular theory of stories that calls this a loose end, and there is an older theory, my father's, the joiner's, that calls it a lock fitted to the one door in the house that is none of the visitors' business.

What was mine was a letter. She brought it down from behind the kitchen crucifix, where it had wintered, addressed in the narrow upright hand: To the man the firm sends.

Six lines.

If you have come this far by the road I left, you have printed the eleven and understood them, and you are an operator, not a clerk. I am sorry for the clerk; the road was made to lose him. The work you are finishing is yours; I finished mine. What remains on the ice is not work and is not the firm's. If you want a hand in it, be at the place the plates were made after the first of August, and come up the west path, alone, in the evening, without the firm in your pockets. If you do not come I will think none the worse. Most debts are best left to the ice. — A.F.

I read it on the breakwater at sunset, with the swifts going over the tower in their thousands and the sea doing its slow bright arithmetic against the stones, as far from ice as the continent could take me, and then I went up and asked the signora what I owed for the week, because the first of August was two days gone already, and the answer to Fell's letter had been decided somewhere north of Genoa days before, riding a night train with its forehead against the glass.

She took my money, most of it, for the room and what she called disturbances. Then at the door in the blue morning she put into my case, wrapped in a page of Il Secolo XIX, flat and stiff, a parting gift: one of the signore's prints, hers to give, she said, since he had made it in her own upstairs hallway and left it as too spoiled to sign.

I unwrapped it on the train. It is before me on the desk as I write this sentence, forty years on. It shows a door standing half open at the end of a whitewashed corridor, morning, the light coming through the gap in one blade across the tiles, and in the lit slice of the room beyond, on the floor, the shadow of a woman, her back to the door, arrested in some plain act, folding perhaps. Nothing else. There is no woman in the pensione it could have been; I asked, that morning, and the signora only looked at the print a while and then at me, and shrugged with her whole body, a shrug the size of the coast, and said what I have since decided is the last word available on the subject.

"He was a long time up there over the winter, signore. Doors are cheap. Light he had plenty. Whoever it is," and she wrapped it back up and tied the string, "she was folding, and content. Take it north where he can't be embarrassed by it."

14. The Season

I came back over the Simplon road on the sixth of August, by diligence from Lake Maggiore, up through the gorges with an opera company bound for the Rhône valley and down the other side with most of their repertoire, and found Zermatt at full boil. August is the crown of the season. The street was a slow river of alpenstocks. The hotels had cots in the reading rooms. The Gornergrat railway, then building, had its cuttings full of Italian laborers and its future full of leader-writers arguing whether a rack railway to a viewpoint was the summit of civilization or its grave, a question the shareholders had already settled to their satisfaction. Six parties a day were going up the Matterhorn by the ropes and ladders of the Hörnli route, at which Edward Whymper's mountain had become, the English climbers said into their soup, a via ferrata for bankers, and the bankers came down sunburnt and alive and tipped like emperors, so the village bore the indignity with fortitude.

Three items of correspondence waited at the Monte Rosa, and I set them down in the order of ascending weather.

The first was from my mother. The school year was arranged; my father had taken a contract for the interior joinery of a new bank in Redlands and pronounced the architect young but curable; Rutherford, of the Coronado, to whom I had sent a card of the Rigi sunrise, had written her, they being by some freemasonry of the trade acquainted, to say her son photographed like a preacher who had seen the place himself. She required of me: flannel next the skin above five thousand feet, the name of the Scottish lady mentioned suspiciously often in my letters, and no heroics. The word heroics was underlined with the same stroke she used for failed grammar.

The second was from Stroud, a night-letter, and I transcribe it faithfully because nothing I could write would render him better:

TWENTY ONE NUMBERS OUTSTANDING SEASON SHORTENING STOP ITALIAN FREIGHT CHARGES NOTED ALSO CHARGES ZMUTT MULES JULY WHAT IS AT ZMUTT REQUIRING ELEVEN MULE DAYS ANSWER STOP NO WORD MERCHANDISE QUERY STOP SPEND LESS EXPLAIN MORE STROUD

The third had no stamp. It had been left at the desk by hand, in an envelope of a quality the village stationer did not stock, and it contained the Colonel's card, MEMPHIS · LUCERNE · THE WORLD, and across the back, in a fine commercial flourish:

Welcome back from the sea, sir. We really must compare notes on the picturesque before the summer gets away from us both. Time and ice wait for no man. — B.K.P.

I stood at the desk reading that twice, and the second reading did what the first had declined to do. Time and ice. It might have been the small talk of the season; the whole village discussed glaciers at dinner as coastal towns discuss tides. It might have been. I went down to my cellar with a lamp before I had taken off my coat.

The lock was where I had left it, and it opened as it had always opened, and that told me what I needed, because I had left it, deliberately, since June, with the shackle turned a half-turn short of home, a joiner's son's habit, my father having taught me that a lock tells you more about your visitors than it refuses them. It was turned fully home now. Some tidy soul had locked up conscientiously on his way out.

Inside, nothing was missing. My stock of plates stood in their cases; the firm's negatives, catalogued and crated for Basle, stood sealed; the eleven prints from Fell's plates hung on the line where any reasonable man would have left drying prints, because a thing on a line looks like process, and hiding it would have made it evidence, and I had reasoned in June that the cellar's best safe was its own boring plainness. I had reasoned like a man with a lock, and not like a man with a visitor who could read. The prints hung in their eleven gray near-identical ranks, and I stood in front of them with the lamp up, trying to see them as a stranger in a hurry would see them, and the exercise steadied me until my eye reached the seventh print, and stopped, and the steadiness went out of the cellar.

They were re-pegged in the wrong order. Not shuffled; re-ordered. Whoever had taken them down had put them back with the care of a man restoring appearances, and he had restored them left to right by the depth of the shadow in the ice cave, darkest to lightest, which is how a picture man sorts prints when the dates scratched on the negatives mean nothing to him and he is working fast, by lamplight, from visual weight. It was a good, professional, fluent restoration, and it was wrong, and it had been wrong for perhaps three weeks while I was in Italy, and the man who had made it had therefore stood where I was standing, long enough to take down eleven prints, study them, and reach whatever an old fraud reaches in place of the truth.

He could not fuse the pairs; there was no viewer in the cellar, and no reason on earth for him to imagine one wanted. He could not read the dates. What he could read, better than any man in the Valais, was the commercial signature of the whole arrangement: a first-class operator exposing eleven plates of a worthless subject and a famous man vanishing rather than explain himself. Prewitt had spent thirty years retailing the mourning of this valley. He knew the story of the unrecovered lord the way a broker knows a bond he has clipped coupons off half his life, and he knew, from the Bossons tale, which every relic man in the Alps kept in stock as patter, that glaciers pay out. He did not have the science, the dates, the window, or the spot. He had eleven gray prints, a welcome-back card, and a nose that had never in thirty years pointed wrong at money, and I judged, standing in my violated cellar, that it was pointing now, and that we no longer owned the only clock in the valley.

Forsyth folded the letter along its creases and put it in the oilcloth satchel with her father's tables, which I judged then and judge now to have been the correct filing, and none of the three of us was fit for talk for a while, and she took the readings at six sharp regardless, because the readings were at six.

The föhn announced itself first in the barometer, then in the animals, then in everything. Herr Imboden tapped the glass at noon on the eighteenth and looked at it the way you look at a debtor's note of hand. By four the sky had gone to a high milky scud with the summits standing in it queerly close and hard-edged, the whole range come forward like the near plane of a badly mounted card. The cows came down off the pastures of their own opinion. The air arrived warm, then warmer, in long dry pushes off the col, smelling of stone, and the old men of the village sat out on the benches sniffing it and naming it to each other with the grim satisfaction of prophets on schedule.

It blew for a night and a day and a second night, and I will not decorate it: a föhn is not a storm out of scripture, only a warm wind with no conscience whatever about snow. It ate the fresh August powder off the whole east face in the first twelve hours and went to work on firn that had thought itself pensioned. The village shutters banged themselves hoarse. The Gornergrat works shut down when a crane went over. Every gutter, every runnel, every stone channel cut by four hundred years of melt ran loud and brown, and under the wind, all the second night, you could hear the valley's true business going forward in the dark: water, more water than the maps allowed for, hurrying down out of ten glaciers toward the young Rhône with the year's whole ration paid out in a night.

Forsyth appeared at the Monte Rosa at first light on the twentieth with the boy asleep on his feet beside her, having been up at her stakes in the tail of the wind, which I forbore to remark on and she forbore to defend.

"Nineteen inches on the mid-line since Thursday," she said. "The tongue has come on more in two days than the first fortnight of July entire. And the arch is down. The south half of the cave mouth collapsed in the night; the river's cutting the north wall as we stand here." She accepted coffee the way the exhausted do, as cargo. "The wall it is cutting, Mr. Joiner, is the wall."

Clemenz came in an hour behind her, down from Staffel at a pace that had opinions of its own, with a note from Fell in the narrow upright hand, and the note said what the note had waited thirty-one years, a stone station, and one warm wind to say, and said it in the only dialect the man had ever fully trusted, which was the shot list's:

Subject at hand. Light wants two days to settle and so does the ice; the wall is shedding. Bring on the 22nd, at first light: Taugwalder, the English chaplain, the lady, the boy if she vouches him, yourself. Litter, sound rope, two lanterns trimmed short. No other soul. Wear your church boots under your work boots. — A.F.

There was a postscript, and the postscript was the man entire, and I have never decided whether it belongs to the comedy of this account or to the other column, and after forty years I let it stand in both:

P.S. The firm's seal on the Basle freight was well struck. You wax a seal like a joiner. Your father's trade? Bring nothing of the firm's on the 22nd, but bring the habit.

The twenty-first we spent on the quiet arrangements such a matter wants, and they are quieter than the reader may suppose, there being a whole grammar of discretion in a village with the scar. The chaplain, informed in the vestry after Evensong, asked one question only, whether the family had been consulted, and being shown the solicitor's paragraph, bowed his head a moment and then went and stood a long time at his shelf deciding which order of burial a man carries to a glacier. Herr Imboden was told nothing and arranged everything, a mule ordered for half past three, a hamper, the yard gate oiled, in the manner of a man conspicuously not asking where his oldest guest's chair had got to. And Peter Taugwalder the younger, met at his own door in the dusk with his supper still on the table behind him, listened to me say it, the ice, the wall, the morning after next, his own two hands wanted at the bottom of the mountain his father never came down from in the village's eyes, and stood in the doorway a while with the lamplight on one side of his face.

"The twenty-second," he said at last. "It is a Sunday."

"The chaplain knows."

"Good," said Taugwalder. "My father was religious." And he went in to his supper, and I walked back up the emptied street to the hotel, and lay a long time listening to the milk-brown water running under everything, and did not trim the lanterns that night, because Fell had said short and my hands wanted daylight for it, and there are tasks a man saves deliberately for the eve of a thing, to have somewhere to put his hands, and that one was mine, and I record that at the last, past midnight, the water's sound came apart the way rain does when you fall asleep in it, into syllables, none of them words.

18. What the Ice Kept

We went up in the dark of Sunday morning, seven of us and the mule: Fell down from Staffel to meet us at the bridge, Forsyth and her boy, Peter Taugwalder, the English chaplain in his traveling cloak with his book and his vessels in a fishing creel, Clemenz leading the mule, and myself. No lantern showed in the village behind us. In the hamlet of Zmutt one window was lit as we passed between the black houses, and an old woman stood in it with a candle, not looking out at us, her head bent, and her hand moving in the small geometry her faith keeps for such hours, and no one spoke there, and the mule's bell had been stuffed with hay by Clemenz before ever we started, an attention I had not thought of and have never forgotten.

Above the hamlet the valley was all one sound of water. The föhn had gone through like a paid bill, and behind it the air had been rinsed to that terrible clarity the south wind leaves, in which the mountain stood up out of the last of the night entire, closer than it has any business standing, and of a blackness that made the starlight around it seem a kind of frost. We walked the crest line as the sky went gray, then ash, then the color of the inside of a shell, and no one said anything worth improving on, which was nothing.

The collapse had changed the whole snout. Where the arch had been there was now a raw amphitheater of ice, and the river went through the ruin of its old roof in a running vault of noise, and the north wall stood over it forty feet, sheared, blue-gray, clean as a cut plum. We came down off the crest single file and stood at the margin, on the outwash gravel, in the place where thirty-one years of arithmetic, two instruments, and one warm wind had appointed to meet, and the light came down the wall to us slowly, the way it does at the bottom of things, and Forsyth's boy, who had carried the second lantern the whole way without being asked, blew it out.

He was in the wall.

I will write this part plainly, and once, and the reader who wants more is directed to the newspapers of that period, which had nothing, and never got anything, and let that be a species of monument too.

The ice at the foot of the wall held, in a lens of clearness the color of bottle glass, at the height of a man's chest, what the mountain had carried down. A climbing boot, nailed, the nails bright where the melt had washed them, which was the grain of light an old operator's fused plates had first hung in a walnut theater fourteen months before. Leather that had been harness and belting, the buckles riding it in the little strung constellation we had watched come due. Cloth pressed to the darkness of wet slate, in folds the ice had kept as the day of the fall had arranged them. The rope. And the rest, which was little, and which was enough, and which I will not further describe except to say that the ice had been gentle by its own standard, and that there was nothing in that wall a mother could not have been told of.

We stood, the seven of us. The chaplain took off his hat first, and then all of us, and the river ran, and it was Ambrose Fell, of all our number, who spoke, and what he said was practical, and was said gently, and I understood at last, hearing it, what thirty-one years had been for, because no man could have said it right without them.

"Slowly, now," he said. "He has been thirty-two years coming down to us. We'll not hurry him in the last ten feet."

The work took four hours, and it was work, and I set down its nature because the truth of such mornings is manual, and the accounts that leave the hands out of it are lying by omission. Taugwalder governed everything. This was his mountain-craft, the taking of weight off ice that grudges it, and he cut and eased and shored with his axe and his hands as his father's son, unhurried, bareheaded the whole four hours, and Clemenz worked as his second and aged five years in the learning. Forsyth kept the record, position and depth and disposition, her voice at dictation level, her boy writing, and her tables lay on a flat stone under the theodolite case with a pencil line already ruled below the last entry, waiting for its figure. The chaplain stood where the chaplain stands. I did what I had been brought for: held, steadied, lifted where a second back was wanted, and measured and witnessed and kept the log of the little that came free and the order of its coming.

The belt was freed at the second hour. Taugwalder washed the buckle in the melt-channel with the motions of a man rinsing communion plate, and carried it to the flat stone, and set it by the tables, and Forsyth turned it once with her pencil's end. The monogram came up under the wet, worn shallow by half a lifetime of a boy's belting, unmistakable in the ash light: F.W.B.D. She looked at it a long moment, and then she wrote the figure into her father's last column, and drew no conclusion on the paper, because the paper was a survey record; and I saw her afterward at the water channel, with her back to us, being longer about rinsing her hands than any hands require.

The rope came free at the third hour, and this is the paragraph I have written most carefully in this whole account, and most often, and I ask the reader to receive it at the pace it was learned.

It was the old line, the thin manila, own brother to the four feet under glass in the Monte Rosa parlor; there was never a moment's question of that. It lay about what remained of the waist in two full turns. Two. The knot, where the melt had polished the ice from it, was doubled, the second knot jammed hard down upon the first, a careful man's finish to a lashing he does not mean to trust to one holding. And the free end, three feet out from the turns, ended in a burst, the fibers drawn long and ragged and every one of them stretched pale at the tip, the signature, any rigger will tell you, any hangman too, of a line that has given way under a load it fought, drawn fiber by fiber past its last strength. Not one strand of it cut. Nothing in it quick, or clean, or chosen. An old rope, doubled by conscience beyond its duty, tied twice about a light man by hands that wanted him kept, and asked one afternoon to hold the falling weight of four, and it had fought, the frayed end said so, it had fought the whole length of itself, and it had lost, and the man who tied it had stood accused in silence for thirty years by every drawing room in Europe of the one thing the knots now swore before witnesses he had never done.

Peter Taugwalder knelt at the turns and read them with his fingers, both hands, as the blind read. He was a long time. The river ran. When he stood up his face was the face he took to church, no other, and he settled his hat back on and said, to the ice wall, in dialect, five or six words I did not need rendered, because the grammar of them was the grammar of a son reporting to a father that a matter left open is now closed. Then he said, in his deliberate English, to the chaplain, as one official to another:

"It is as it was testified. Write it in your language also."

And the chaplain, who kept the register of the English church, and who understood entirely what was being asked of him and by how many generations, answered, "It will be written," and it was, that evening, in Latin, in a hand as good as any caption department's, in an entry I have seen once and will render at its proper place in this account.

The sun cleared the eastern ridges at half past nine and came down the full height of the north wall, and the ice went from gray to white to a burning ordinary daylight blue, and by then the litter was made, and covered, and corded, Taugwalder at the head of it, and what the mountain had kept for thirty-two years began, at last, at the pace of the mule, the last four miles of the longest descent in the history of that valley.

Fell had carried his camera up on his own back, the great field instrument, and one plate holder, one, and all the four hours of the work it had stood cased against a boulder like a thing left at a door, and I had begun to believe, and I confess a part of me had begun to hope, that he would not uncase it at all. He uncased it as the party formed to move off. He did not go near the wall. He set his tripod on the outwash, low, forty yards down the going-away line of the path, and I saw the composition assemble itself in the world as he made his three motions, and I want it understood that I saw it before the exposure and not after, because it is the last thing in my trade I ever learned entirely new, and I learned it standing in it.

He had not come thirty-one years to photograph what the ice kept. He had come to photograph what the men did. The plate, when he pulled the slide, held this and only this: the bearing party small on the great gray page of the moraine, the litter and its bearers, guide and chaplain and lady and boys, walking down away from the lens into the enormous morning, and over them the ice wall gone to daylight, and over that the mountain, four miles of it, standing back the way it stands in every card the parlors of two continents ever owned, scenery again, weather again, with the witness at last walking home out of the bottom of the view. One exposure. He capped the lens, and looked a moment at the ground glass gone blind, and said, in the voice of a man closing the last ledger of a long firm, "There's the head, then," and cased the camera, and took his corner of the litter's relief rotation like any other pair of shoulders on the payroll of that morning.

We came down through Zmutt at noon, and the hamlet stood at its doors bareheaded, all eleven souls of it, having known everything always, in the way of such places, and told nothing, in the way of such places; and as the path turned at the last black barn the bells of the village church far below started up for the Sunday, the plain daily peal, ringing the valley to its dinner as they had rung it every noon for four hundred years, and would tomorrow, and did.

19. The Colonel's Last Sale

He met us below Zmutt, where the path comes down through the larches and a man standing in the shade of them commands the way as well as any customs house, and he had brought three men up with him: two porters engaged at the lower end of the village, and a narrow young man in a dust coat whom I identified at forty yards, by the tripod on his shoulder, as a photographer, and at twenty, by the newness of the tripod, as a Geneva man hired by the week. The Colonel himself stood forward in the path in the pale suit, hat in hand before the party in an undertaker's posture I do not doubt he had rehearsed, and he let the mule and the litter come nearly to him before he spoke, and when he spoke the molasses was at full pour.

"Gentlemen. Ladies. A solemn morning, and I would not profane it. But there is such a thing as the public's sacred interest in a matter thirty years its own, and there is such a thing, sirs, as history's claim, and I stand here, humbly, in history's name—"

"Stand out of history's road," said Alexandra Forsyth, from beside the litter, in the voice that had cleared moraines, and I believe with hindsight that the whole affair might have been concluded by that sentence alone, for the Colonel's porters, who were valley men, had already seen what the litter was. They had their hats off before she finished, both of them, off and against their chests, and they backed out of the path and up onto the bank with their eyes down, the way men leave a church pew, and no wage on earth was going to put them back in the road, and a colonel without porters in that country is a man in a pale suit standing alone at seven thousand feet, a long way from Memphis.

He held his ground regardless, I will give the old buzzard that much for the record, and he shifted to the flank where his strength was. "Then I'll trouble nobody's feelings here," he said, sweeping the hat toward the valley, the wire office, the world. "The story tells itself by telegraph, with me or without me, and London pays by the question. You know my Saturday terms, Mr. Joiner. They have ripened."

"Before you wire London, Colonel," said Ambrose Fell, "ask your dispatch case what it's carrying."

I had wondered how the Colonel would meet Fell, the legend he had traded on, dealt with in small dealings, and plainly not expected ever to see standing on a mountain path behind a litter. What he did was go still, the whole performance of him, for two full seconds, which for such a man is a public confession, and then reassemble the warmth several sizes smaller.

"Ambrose. They said you were dead."

"They said you were a colonel. Open the case."

He did not open the case. His thumb went to the latch of it, one small involuntary tenderness, the way a man's tongue goes to a doubtful tooth, and that told everyone in the path everything about where the buckle rode.

"You bought it Tuesday," said Fell, in the dry level voice of a man reading an invoice aloud. "Eleven francs, beaten down from thirty, off a shepherd too poor to argue. Silver-gilt, monogrammed to the correct initials, gravel in the keeper, glacier polish on the face. I'll tell you what your Geneva man there can tell you tonight with a loupe if you stand him a lamp: the underside carries a Birmingham assay mark, and the date letter is 1871. Six years after the fall, Colonel. The finest expert in London will read it in under a minute, and so will the second finest, and they are both of them on the first train the moment you wire your question, because your forty correspondents don't travel alone. You've spent thirty years keeping this valley's grief at a simmer, out of the daylight, in velvet cases, where no man of learning ever looks twice. Send that wire, and daylight arrives by the trainload, and it examines everything, Colonel. The relic in your case. The certificates in your ledger. The four hundred feet you've retailed of a two-hundred-foot rope. You are the one man in Europe who cannot afford the crowd you're threatening us with, and you have been that man since Tuesday, and you paid eleven francs for the privilege."

The path was quiet. Somewhere down the valley the Sunday bells were still at it. Colonel Byron K. Prewitt stood in the larch shade doing sums, visibly, rapidly, an old machine and no rust anywhere in it, whatever else was wrong with the works, and I watched him arrive at the remainder, check it once, and accept the figure, all inside ten seconds, and I confess a purely technical admiration. I have seen honest men take a week to swallow less.

"The shepherd," he said at last, meditatively, to no one, in the dry Memphis voice. "Gravel in the keeper. He wept at fifteen francs, that boy. Wept." He looked past us all at Clemenz, at the head of the mule, whose face was a monument of alpine innocence, and something moved under the silver mustache that had every appearance of professional salute, one horse trader to the stable that had finally sold him one. "Beautiful," said the Colonel. "Beautiful."

He put his hat back on and squared it, and made his last offer with the trim of a man conceding a hand but not the table, because such men do not own another manner and it is idle to expect one at their age.

"There'd still be a fortune in it, done respectful," he said. "Books closed, twenty years hence. Memoirs. The Glacier's Secret, by One Who Was Present."

"Colonel," I said, "you weren't present."

He inclined the hat a half inch, granting the point as a debating matter merely, and stood aside off the path, because whatever else the man knew, he knew a closed market at sight. And as the litter came past him, Byron Prewitt took the hat off again, all the way off, and held it to his chest until the mule and its burden and the whole silent company had gone down out of the larches, and I would set it down as performance and be done, except that his eyes at that moment were on none of us who could be performed at, and a lifetime in the reading of faces obliges me to report that for the length of that passing his own had gone empty of salesmanship entire, and old, and I do not know what was in it, and I have made it a rule in this account to say so when I do not know.

The rest of the Colonel is quickly told. He came down to the village that afternoon and was met at the door of his pension by its keeper, with his trunks already corded in the passage behind her, the village having concluded its own proceedings that morning by the means villages own; and at the Monte Rosa desk, where he presented himself with some notion of a farewell drink on the veranda, Herr Imboden regarded him across the register for a moment and then said, in English, pleasantly, with the finality of a barometer, "We have no season for you, sir. Nor has Lucerne," and that second clause was the true sentence of execution, for the hotel men of that country are a lodge, and word had gone down the valley wires before the Sunday bells stopped, and a memorial-goods man the Seiler list has closed its verandas to is a man whose trade is over from Chur to Chamonix, and both of them knew it, and no voice was raised at all.

He left on the Tuesday morning train. I was on the platform, seeing off the Basle freight, and he came along it in the pale suit with his cases on a barrow and stopped, because such men always stop, an exit unwitnessed being to them a form of death.

"Your Geneva boy," I said, "was owed a week he'll now not work. I've bought his plates and his time; the firm can use a second camera at the Gornergrat works. You'll want to settle his hotel, though."

"Settled at dawn," said the Colonel, wounded. "I have never in my life stiffed labor. Capital, yes; labor, never. A man must bank somewhere." He looked up the street, past the hotels, to where the mountain stood in its Tuesday morning weather, ordinary as bread, with two ravens rowing across the face of it, and for a moment I thought the emptiness might come back into his face, but the works had recovered themselves, and what came instead was the trade voice, softened one register, the nearest thing to a confession the machinery could produce. "Thirty years I retailed that mountain's sorrow, Mr. Joiner, and I'll tell you the queer part free of charge, as one operator to another: there was never a season the demand slackened. Never one. The stock of grief in this world," said Colonel Byron K. Prewitt, stepping up into the carriage and settling the planter's hat for the descent to Visp, "is the only inventory that replenishes itself, and the only one I ever handled that I couldn't water."

The train took him down the valley, and the valley, I am able to report across the distance of forty years, has never once missed him, and neither have I, and I have thought of him often, which is a different thing, and the distinction is the most he is owed.

20. Discretion

He was buried on the Monday, at night, which sounds furtive and was the opposite: it was the only hour the village could attend without the season attending too. Word had gone through Zermatt's own channels, below the hotel floors, in the kitchens and the guides' room and the stables, that the thing would be at eleven, and that it was the village's and no one else's, and I never learned that a single soul of the four thousand visitors then sleeping above those kitchens ever knew. The English churchyard at eleven held the seven of us, the chaplain now properly in his surplice, Herr Imboden with his staff of six standing behind him like a family, the pastor of the Swiss church, come in his cassock to stand at his colleague's shoulder on the theory, which he stated at the gate in French and did not argue, that a valley has one register in heaven whatever it keeps on earth, and then, coming quietly through the gate in twos and threes until they stood three deep along the wall, the guides. The black coats. I counted upward of forty, which was every man of the company then in the valley and some down from the huts who cannot have heard before eight o'clock, and not one of them had been sent for. They stood along the wall with their hats off and their ropes coiled on their shoulders, in the full dress of their trade, as they stand for one of their own, and Peter Taugwalder stood at the head of the grave, and his son, who was twelve, held the lantern.

The grave was dug beside his companions of that day. The chaplain read the order of burial entire, hurrying nothing, and where the office says the name he said only, in a steady voice, "our brother, whom God has known these two and thirty years by name," and the mountain stood over the churchyard in the starlight, enormous, half the sky's stars swallowed by the black of it, and when the first earth went down the guides along the wall did the thing I have never told without stopping a moment first. They rang their axes. Each man as the earth fell struck his ice axe head once, softly, against the churchyard wall's stone coping, one after another down the line, forty small clear strokes like a peal rung handwise, the sound their trade makes on a thousand mornings on the ice, going up; and that was the passing bell they gave him, the noise of the craft that had taken him and kept him and carried him down, and then it was quiet, and the earth went on falling, and that was all.

The rope went into the grave with him. That was settled the evening before, in the vestry, in a conference of ten minutes I was called into as a witness of the recovery, and it was Taugwalder's word that settled it. The chaplain had raised, with delicacy, the question of the knots: whether the rope ought not to be kept, deposited, attested, it being, he said, evidence, and the answer of Peter Taugwalder, delivered after his customary silence, in his deliberate English, was this: "Evidence is for a trial. There is no trial. There was never a trial, only talk. You cannot bury talk, Herr Kaplan, I have tried thirty years. But a rope you can bury." And then, laying his hand flat on the vestry table: "It held him twice about. Let it keep doing its work."

What was kept was paper. The chaplain drew up that night, and we signed before midnight, all seven, an attestation of what the ice had rendered and the condition of every article of it, the two turns, the doubled knot, the drawn burst of the parting, each particular set out in the dry actuarial English such documents want, and the document was sealed with the solicitor's letter into the parish safe, to lie there against the day, if it ever dawns, that print decides to retract a whisper. It lies there now. And the register of the English church at Zermatt carries, under the twenty-third of August, 1897, four words of Latin in the chaplain's small strong hand, and I copy them here as the whole public monument of that summer, and as sufficient:

Advena, monti redditus. Deo notus.

A stranger, given back by the mountain. Known to God.

As for the village, no proclamation was ever made, none being the village's way. What happened instead, Clemenz reported to me, was one sentence in the guides' room on the Tuesday morning, when the talk paused on some ordinary matter of tariffs, and old Perren of the committee looked across at Taugwalder and said, in dialect, gruffly, as such men pay thirty years of debt, "Your father tied well, Peter," and the room said nothing at all, in the way a room says amen, and the talk went on to tariffs, and that was the end of the slander of Peter Taugwalder the elder in the one jurisdiction where it had ever really been tried.

"I was there," said Fell.

The dowager marchioness thanked the daughter of Professor Forsyth for the survey record, which she had read, she wrote, entire, twice, with her spectacles and then with her glass, and found more comfort in than in most sermons, exactness being, at her age, the only tenderness left that did not condescend. A Note on the History

Readers of historical fiction deserve to be told where the joinery is, so here is the inventory.

The frame of this story is real. On 14 July 1865, Edward Whymper, Lord Francis Douglas, the Rev. Charles Hudson, Douglas Hadow, the guide Michel Croz, and Peter Taugwalder father and son made the first ascent of the Matterhorn. On the descent Hadow slipped; Croz, Hudson, Hadow, and Douglas fell the height of the north face; the rope parted between Douglas and the elder Taugwalder. The rope that broke was in fact the weakest line the party carried, and the question of why it was in use at that interval was examined by an official inquiry, which cleared Taugwalder. The insinuation that he had chosen or cut it pursued him anyway, for the rest of his life. He spent some years in America before returning to Zermatt, and died in 1888. His son Peter guided for decades more. The bodies of Croz, Hudson, and Hadow were recovered from the Matterhorn glacier days after the fall and lie in Zermatt. Lord Francis Douglas, nineteen years old, was never found, then or since; only a few effects came down. His mother lived into the twentieth century. His elder brother was the Marquess of Queensberry whose quarrels filled the newspapers of the 1890s, which is fact, and is as far as this book presumes to lean on it. A portion of the broken rope is preserved in Zermatt to this day.

The science is real in kind and adjusted in detail. Glaciers flow, and their rates were the passion of Victorian physicists from J. D. Forbes onward. The precedent cited by Miss Forsyth is genuine: the remains of guides lost in the Hamel accident on Mont Blanc in 1820 emerged at the foot of the Glacier des Bossons some forty years later, roughly where and when the theory of glacier motion said they would. Bodies of climbers have continued to emerge from Alpine ice on schedules of decades ever since, and the Matterhorn's glaciers have returned other men in other years. They have not returned Douglas. Professor David Forsyth, his daughter, his tables, and his window are inventions, built from the honest materials of the era's glaciology. Whether ice below the Matterhorn's north face could carry a burden to its snout in thirty-two years is a question I have answered as a novelist, which is to say: within the window of the plausible, and past the edge of the proven.

The trade is real. Stereoscopic view publishing was one of the great popular industries of the age; firms like Underwood & Underwood sent salaried operators across the world and sold the results door to door, a hundred cards to the box, and "Sun Sculpture" was a genuine trade name of the era for photographs so published. Operators were, as a rule, not credited; the firm was the artist. Stroud Brothers, E. O. Stroud, Considine, Ambrose Fell, and Hollis Joiner are inventions, though the vault of glass, the caption departments, the college-boy canvassers, and the letters asking after a dog's name in negative forty-one are all true to the business. The technique at the heart of the book, viewing two photographs taken from one station at different times so that whatever has changed stands out in false relief, is real, and had scientific careers ahead of it in astronomy (where the blink and stereo comparators found asteroids and Pluto) and in glacier photogrammetry, which was being practiced in the Alps by the 1890s. I know of no evidence that a view-company operator privately invented time-parallax at Zermatt in 1896, and I know of no evidence that one did not, which is the exact climate in which fiction grows best.

Zermatt in 1897 is drawn as the records show it: the railway from Visp new, the Gornergrat rack line building, the Seiler hotels in their pomp, the Monte Rosa keeping the registers of the whole sport's history, the guides in their black coats, the föhn doing what the föhn does. The Hotel del Coronado, the orange valleys of Highland and Redlands, the Rigi sunrise with its alp-horn and blankets, Vernazza and its terraces, and the Théodule crossing are likewise drawn from the period's own accounts. The Black regiments of Massachusetts are history; so are the finish carpenters who came out of them, and the freedmen who chose their surnames from their trades; so is the arithmetic, in that America, of a hotel's hospitality. Winnetou was at that date already the most beloved American in the German language, and had never seen America, which seemed too true to leave out.

Colonel Prewitt is invented, with regret that he has to be, since his profession was not: the relic trade, the memorial-portrait trade, and spirit photography all flourished wherever grief gathered, and the Alps gathered a great deal of it. The buckle's hallmark trick I commend to no one.

What the ice gives back in this book, it has never given back in fact. That is the hinge on which the whole invention turns, and I have tried to turn it quietly: in this story the world never learns, the newspapers never come, the register keeps four words of Latin, and history outside the valley is left exactly as the reader found it, which is the only license by which a novelist should be permitted to borrow a real grief. The dead of the Matterhorn were real men. The mountain still keeps one of them. This book is for the people who waited, and for everyone whose work went out into the world, for a hundred years of parlor Sundays, under somebody else's name.
